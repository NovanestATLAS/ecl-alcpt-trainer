30 common grammar mistakes that cost ECL and ALCPT points

For each mistake: what learners often say, what to say instead, a short reason and a link to the full lesson. At the end, a simple routine to stop making the same mistake twice.

Updated October 18, 2026

Why small mistakes cost points

The ECL and the ALCPT are multiple-choice tests of listening and reading in American English. In a multiple-choice grammar question, the wrong choices usually look like real learner mistakes. That is what makes them dangerous. If you say "We have finished the report yesterday" at work every week, that choice will look normal to you on test day.

Here is a short sample item:

We ______ the report yesterday afternoon.
A. have finished
B. finished
C. finish
D. are finishing

The answer is B. Yesterday afternoon is a finished time, so the sentence needs the simple past. Choice A is the trap, and it is the first mistake on the list below.

This guide groups 30 common mistakes into seven families. Each one shows what learners often say (Not), what to say instead (Say), a short reason and, often, a second example. Each section ends with a link to the full lesson. To get the most from it, cover the Say line and try to fix the sentence yourself before you read on.

Tenses

Before you choose a tense, find the words that tell you about time: yesterday, since, as soon as, by the time, while. They usually decide the answer.

  1. Not: My wife has called the clinic two days ago. Say: My wife called the clinic two days ago.
    A finished time (ago, yesterday, last week, in 2022, on Monday) needs the simple past. Use the present perfect when the time is not finished or not mentioned: She has called the clinic three times this week.
  2. Not: I am in this unit since 2021. Say: I have been in this unit since 2021.
    For something that started in the past and is still true now, use the present perfect with since (the starting point) or for (the length of time). Do not mix up the two little words: for three years, never since three years. Also: We have been waiting for the shuttle for twenty minutes.
  3. Not: I will text you as soon as the plane will land. Say: I will text you as soon as the plane lands.
    When when, as soon as, before, after or until starts a time clause, use the present simple for the future action. Will belongs only in the other half of the sentence: Please don't start until everyone arrives. In a direct question, will is fine: When will you land?
  4. Not: By the time we got to the gym, the class already started. Say: By the time we got to the gym, the class had already started.
    When one past action was complete before another past moment, use the past perfect (had + past participle) for the earlier action. By the time and already are strong clues. Also: My parents had never been on a plane before they visited us.
  5. Not: When my son called from the airport, I cooked dinner. Say: When my son called from the airport, I was cooking dinner.
    Use the past continuous (was or were + -ing) for an action that was already in progress, and the simple past for the short action that interrupted it. With two simple past verbs, the sentence means you started cooking after the call. Also: The power went out while we were having lunch.

On the test: read the whole sentence before you look at the choices, because the time word often comes at the end. Every tense, with its form and signal words, is in the verb tenses lesson.

Verb patterns: gerund or infinitive

Some verbs are followed by -ing, some by to + verb, and a few by the base form alone. You can't always guess the pattern, so learn each verb together with its partner: enjoy doing, decide to do, let someone do.

  1. Not: Please avoid to park in front of the dining facility. Say: Please avoid parking in front of the dining facility.
    Avoid, enjoy, finish, mind, keep, practice, suggest and consider are followed by -ing. Also: Do you mind waiting a few minutes?
  2. Not: We decided taking the night train to Chicago. Say: We decided to take the night train to Chicago.
    Decide, want, plan, hope, agree, promise, refuse and need are followed by to + base form. Also: My brother promised to help us move.
  3. Not: I look forward to meet your family. Say: I look forward to meeting your family.
    After a preposition, a verb takes -ing. In look forward to, be used to and get used to, the word to is a preposition, not part of the verb: She is used to working nights. Don't confuse this with used to + base form, which describes an old habit: I used to work nights.
  4. Not: My boss let me to leave early on Friday. Say: My boss let me leave early on Friday.
    After make or let + a person, use the base form with no to. Help works both ways: help me carry or help me to carry. Also: The rain made us cancel the picnic.

On the test: look at the verb just before the blank. That verb decides the form, not the meaning of the sentence. The full lists are in the gerunds and infinitives lesson.

Articles and quantifiers

Small words like a, the, much and many are easy to skip, especially if your first language has no articles or uses them differently. Ask two questions about the noun: can I count it, and do I mean one specific thing or things in general?

  1. Not: My sister is nurse at the base hospital. Say: My sister is a nurse at the base hospital.
    A singular countable noun needs a word in front of it (a, the, my, this), and jobs take a or an. Choose by the first sound, not the first letter: an hour, an MRI, a uniform, a one-way ticket.
  2. Not: The smartphones have changed the family life. Say: Smartphones have changed family life.
    When you talk about things in general, use a plural or uncountable noun with no article. Use the for specific ones that both people can identify: The phones in this room must stay off. Meals and sports usually take no article either: after lunch, play soccer.
  3. Not: All our luggages are in the car. Say: All our luggage is in the car.
    Some nouns are uncountable in English: luggage, advice, information, equipment, furniture, homework, news. They take no -s, no a or an, and a singular verb. To count them, add a unit: two pieces of luggage, a piece of advice.
  4. Not: There were too much people at the airport. Say: There were too many people at the airport.
    Use many and a few with plural nouns (many people, a few days), and much and a little with uncountable nouns (much time, a little money). A lot of works with both. Also: How many times have you flown this year?

On the test: when the choices are much, many, few and little, look at the noun after the blank before you decide. The rules and the plural spellings are in the articles and quantifiers lesson.

Prepositions

Prepositions rarely translate word for word from your language. Learn them in chunks, together with the words around them: on the weekend, wait for, married to.

  1. Not: We usually go to the lake in the weekend. Say: We usually go to the lake on the weekend.
    Use at for clock times (at 6:30, at noon, at night), on for days and dates (on Friday, on May 3), and in for longer periods (in July, in 2025, in the morning). Both tests use American English, so learn on the weekend, not the British at the weekend.
  2. Not: We arrived to Atlanta after midnight. Say: We arrived in Atlanta after midnight.
    There is no arrive to. Say arrive in a city or country and arrive at a building or a place: arrive at the hotel, arrive at the gate. And home needs no preposition at all: arrive home, go home, I got home at six.
  3. Not: Can you wait me at the main gate? Say: Can you wait for me at the main gate?
    Some verbs need a fixed preposition before their object: wait for, listen to, look at, ask for, pay for, depend on. Others take none, even if your language uses one: We discussed the plan (not discussed about), She entered the room, Call me tonight.
  4. Not: My cousin is married with a pilot. Say: My cousin is married to a pilot.
    Adjectives have fixed partners too: married to, interested in, good at, afraid of, proud of, responsible for, different from. Learn the pair, not the adjective alone. Also: Who is responsible for the keys to the van?

On the test: when several prepositions seem possible, choose the one you have seen with that exact word, not the one your own language would use. Lists by type are in the prepositions lesson.

Conditionals and modals

Conditionals follow fixed tense patterns, and modal verbs follow a few strict rules. In both, one extra word (will, would or to) is enough to make a sentence wrong.

  1. Not: If the meeting will end early, I'll call you. Say: If the meeting ends early, I'll call you.
    For a real possibility in the future, use the present simple after if, and will, can or an instruction in the other half. It is the same rule as when and as soon as in the tenses section. Also: If the bus is full, we'll take a taxi.
  2. Not: If I would live closer to the base, I would walk to work. Say: If I lived closer to the base, I would walk to work.
    In a conditional, would belongs in the result half. For an unreal present, use if + past simple. For an unreal past, use if + past perfect with would have + past participle: If we had left earlier, we would have caught the bus. (The polite if you would like is a fixed exception.)
  3. Not: You should to see a doctor about that cough. Say: You should see a doctor about that cough.
    After can, could, will, would, should, must, may and might, use the base form: no to, no -s, no -ing. So: My father can fix almost anything (not can fixes). Have to, ought to and be able to are different: they keep their to.
  4. Not: The Saturday class is optional, so you must not come. Say: The Saturday class is optional, so you don't have to come.
    These two look similar but mean very different things. Must not means it is not allowed. Don't have to means it is not necessary, so you can choose. Also: You must not park in front of the fire station.
  5. Not: I should study more before the last test. Say: I should have studied more before the last test.
    To talk about the past, a modal needs have + past participle: should have studied (a regret), must have left (a strong guess), could have helped (a chance that was not used). Also: Her car isn't here, so she must have left already.

On the test: in an if sentence, check both halves before you choose, because the tense of one half decides the other. More examples are in the conditionals lesson and the modal verbs lesson.

Questions and word order

English word order is strict. In most questions, a helping verb such as do, did, is, have or can comes before the subject. In statements, the subject comes first and the object stays close to its verb.

  1. Not: Did you received my email about the schedule? Say: Did you receive my email about the schedule?
    When you use do, does or did, that word carries the tense and the -s, so the main verb stays in the base form. The same goes for negatives: She didn't call (not didn't called), He doesn't know (not doesn't knows).
  2. Not: Could you tell me what time does the shuttle leave? Say: Could you tell me what time the shuttle leaves?
    After Do you know, Can you tell me, I wonder and I'm not sure, the question becomes part of a longer sentence. It takes normal statement order and no do. Also: I don't know where she parked the car.
  3. Not: You're on duty this weekend, isn't it? Say: You're on duty this weekend, aren't you?
    A question tag repeats the helping verb and the subject of the sentence. It is not a fixed isn't it. A positive sentence takes a negative tag, and a negative sentence takes a positive one: Your sister lives in Denver, doesn't she? They haven't eaten yet, have they?
  4. Not: My husband likes very much basketball. Say: My husband likes basketball very much.
    Keep the verb and its object together, and put very much, well and time words after the object: He speaks English very well. Frequency words (always, usually, never) go before the main verb but after be: I always check my email first. She is never late.

On the test: a sentence can have every word right and still be wrong because of the order, so read each choice slowly. Practice the patterns in the questions and tags lesson.

Agreement, pronouns and other small words

These mistakes are tiny, which is why they are hard to hear in your own English. Find the real subject of the sentence, then check that the verb and the pronouns match it.

  1. Not: Everyone in my office have a parking pass. Say: Everyone in my office has a parking pass.
    Everyone, nobody, each and every take a singular verb. So does one of + plural noun, because the subject is one: One of my friends lives in Texas. Watch for words between the subject and the verb: The keys to the van are on the desk.
  2. Not: It has a pharmacy next to the commissary. Say: There is a pharmacy next to the commissary.
    To say that something exists in a place, English uses there is (singular) or there are (plural), not it has. The verb agrees with the noun that follows: There are two flights to Dallas every day.
  3. Not: My wife and me are visiting her parents in May. Say: My wife and I are visiting her parents in May.
    Use I, he, she, we and they as the subject, and me, him, her, us and them after a verb or a preposition. A quick test: take away the other person. You would say I am visiting, so my wife and I. You would say send the photos to me, so send the photos to my wife and me.
  4. Not: The hotel was too clean, so we will stay there again. Say: The hotel was very clean, so we will stay there again.
    Too means more than is good or possible, so it usually brings a problem: The hotel was too expensive, so we stayed somewhere else. For a positive meaning, use very or really. And put enough after an adjective but before a noun: warm enough, enough chairs.

On the test: skip the words between the subject and the verb in your head, then read the sentence again. Pronoun forms and words like another and other are explained in the pronouns lesson.

How to stop repeating a mistake

Reading the right form once rarely fixes a habit. If you have said "I am in this unit since 2021" for years, your eyes will go back to it under time pressure. You need a short routine that brings the same point back again and again.

1. Keep a mistake notebook

Every time a teacher, a test or the trainer shows you a mistake, write it down the same day. Use four columns and keep each entry short:

DateWhat I wrote or saidCorrect formWhy, in my own words
March 3We have finished it yesterday.We finished it yesterday.Finished time, so simple past.
March 5Can you tell me where is Room 12?Can you tell me where Room 12 is?A question inside a sentence keeps normal order.

Under each entry, add one true sentence about your own life that uses the correct form. A sentence about your own family, job or travel will stay in your memory longer than an example from a page.

2. Let the mistake box bring it back

In the free trainer, every question you miss goes into a mistake box. It comes back after 1, 3 and 7 days, so you meet it again just when you are starting to forget it. Clear your mistake box each day before you start new questions.

3. Re-test yourself

One week after you add a mistake, cover the right-hand columns of your notebook and correct the left column out loud. Then take a short timed test of 20 questions in the trainer and check whether the same family of mistakes comes back. If it does, go back to the lesson. If it doesn't, keep the entry but move on. Every week or two, one of the 80 numbered practice tests will show you which families still cost you points.

4. Use it the same day

Write the correct form in a real email, a text message or a conversation before the day ends. Using a structure for something you actually need is what turns it from a rule you know into English you speak.

5. Work on one family at a time

Nobody can fix thirty mistakes at once. Pick the family that appears most often in your notebook, spend a week on it, and only then move to the next one. Small, steady fixes add up, and every mistake you stop making is one less trap on test day.
