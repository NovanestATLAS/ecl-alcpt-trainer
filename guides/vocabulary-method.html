How to learn English vocabulary for the ECL and ALCPT

Choose the right words, learn them in sentences, review them on a schedule and test yourself out loud. With a 15-minute daily routine, a four-week plan and simple ways to track your progress.

Updated October 18, 2026

Why vocabulary needs a method

The ECL and the ALCPT are multiple-choice tests of listening and reading in American English. Grammar matters, but you need words for every question. If you miss one key word in a recording, the whole question can go wrong. If you don't know a word in a sentence, all four choices start to look possible.

Many learners study words the hard way. They copy long lists, read them again and again, and forget most of them a week later. The problem is not their memory. It is the method.

This guide gives you a method in four steps:

  1. Choose the words you will really meet.
  2. Learn each word in a sentence, together with the words that go with it.
  3. Review it on a schedule, just before you forget it.
  4. Test yourself out loud instead of just reading.

Which words to learn first

Not all words are equal. A word you hear every day at work is worth more than ten rare words from a dictionary. Start with three groups.

Frequent everyday words

These are the words of normal life: time, places, health, shopping, travel and work. Think of appointment, available, delay, receipt, refund, schedule and symptom. You probably half-know many of them. Your goal is to know them fast, without stopping to think.

Service words

If you work in or with the military, add the words of base life and training: barracks, formation, duty roster, briefing, inspection, supplies and commanding officer. Some are normal words with a special meaning on base. She is on leave until Monday means she has official time off, not that she left. Test yourself on words like these with the military terms questions.

American usage

The tests use American English. If you learned British English at school, some everyday words will be different. Learn the American word first.

American EnglishBritish EnglishExample
vacationholidayWe're taking a vacation in July.
apartmentflatTheir apartment is ten minutes from the main gate.
gaspetrolLet's stop for gas before we get on the highway.
linequeueThere's a long line at the post office.
checkbillCan we get the check, please?

Americans also write the month first, so 6/10 means June 10. Other pairs to know: elevator (lift), trash (rubbish) and pants (trousers).

A good first list is the ECL vocabulary by theme lesson. It sorts useful words into themes such as life on base, travel, health and work. Each word has a meaning and an example, and every theme has its own flashcards and quiz.

Learn words in sentences, not lists

A list tells you what a word means. It does not tell you how to use it. Look at this list entry:

available = free to use, or free to talk

Now look at the same word in three sentences:

  • The conference room is available after 1400.
  • Is Captain Ortiz available for a quick call?
  • Sorry, that size isn't available in this store.

The sentences teach you more. You see that available comes after be, that people and things can both be available, and that it often goes with for. You also get a picture in your head, and pictures are easier to remember than definitions.

Make cards with a gap

Write a sentence on the front of a card, with the new word missing. Put the word, its meaning and its partners on the back.

  • Front: The clinic is closed on Saturday, so I made an ______ for Monday at 0800.
  • Back: appointment = a fixed time to see a doctor or an official. Goes with: make, have, cancel an appointment.

A gap makes you think about meaning and grammar at the same time. Even better, write the sentence about your own life: We have PT (physical training) at 0600, so I set my alarm for 0520. A true sentence about your job, your family or your base is easier to remember than one from a book.

Collocations: words that go together

A collocation is a group of words that native speakers use together. You make a mistake, but you do your homework. You take a break, but you have lunch. There is often no logic to it, so learn the whole chunk. Don't learn decision. Learn make a decision.

VerbCommon partnersExample
makea decision, a mistake, a reservation, a phone callI made a reservation for six people on Friday night.
doyour best, the laundry, paperwork, push-ups, someone a favorCould you do me a favor and sign for my package?
takea break, a test, notes, a seat, medicineTake this medicine twice a day with food.
givea briefing, a presentation, advice, someone a rideThe sergeant will give a short briefing at 0700.
payattention, a bill, a visitPay attention to the speed limit on base.
catcha cold, a bus, a flightI caught a cold on the flight home.

Adjectives and nouns have partners too. Here are three common mistakes:

  • Not: There was strong rain all night. Say: There was heavy rain all night.
  • Not: He has a strong fever. Say: He has a high fever.
  • Not: Can you make a photo of us? Say: Can you take a photo of us?

Note that American English says make a decision. Take a decision is mostly British. Also write the preposition on the card, because it is part of the word: responsible for, depend on, interested in, afraid of.

Word families: one root, several words

Many English words come in families. Learn the family together and you get three or four words for the price of one. You also learn which form fits where.

VerbNounAdjectiveAdverb
relyreliabilityreliable, unreliablereliably
decidedecisiondecisivedecisively
succeedsuccesssuccessfulsuccessfully
carecarecareful, carelesscarefully
preparepreparationprepared(none)

This helps on multiple-choice questions, because the choices are sometimes four forms of the same word. Here is a short sample item:

The new radios are much more ______ than the old ones.
(A) rely (B) reliable (C) reliability (D) reliably

Answer: B. The sentence needs an adjective to describe the radios: more reliable than. The tempting wrong choice is reliably, but that is an adverb. Adverbs describe actions: The radios work reliably.

Word endings give you clues:

  • Nouns often end in -tion, -ment, -ness or -ity: preparation, equipment, fitness, security.
  • Adjectives often end in -able, -ful, -ous or -ive: reliable, careful, dangerous, effective.
  • Adverbs usually end in -ly: quickly, safely. But friendly and lonely are adjectives.
  • Verbs often end in -ize, -ify or -en: organize, identify, shorten.

Watch the spelling, too: maintain becomes maintenance, and pronounce becomes pronunciation.

Confusable words: learn them as pairs

Some words look or sound almost the same. On a multiple-choice test, that is what makes them dangerous. Learn them side by side, each with its own example.

  • affect / effect. Affect is usually a verb: The storm affected our flight. Effect is usually a noun: The new schedule had a good effect on the team. Trick: Affect is the Action, Effect is the End result.
  • lose / loose. Lose is a verb, and it ends with a z sound: Don't lose your ID card. Loose means "not tight," and it rhymes with goose: These boots are too loose.
  • advice / advise. Advice is a noun: She gave me good advice. Advise is a verb: The doctor advised me to rest.
  • personal / personnel. Personal means private: a personal phone call. Personnel means the people who work in an organization: All personnel must attend the safety briefing.
  • borrow / lend. You borrow from someone, and you lend to someone: Can I borrow your charger? Can you lend me your charger?
  • quiet / quite. Quiet means "not noisy": The barracks are quiet after 2200. Quite means "fairly": The drive was quite long.

Watch out for false friends

Some English words look like words in your own language but mean something else. Actually means "in fact," not "at the moment." Eventually means "in the end," not "maybe." Attend means "go to," not "wait." When an English word looks familiar, check it before you trust it.

Phrasal verbs and idioms

Phrasal verbs are verbs with a small word after them, like up, out, off or in. They are everywhere in spoken American English. People often say fill out the form instead of complete the form, and call off the meeting instead of cancel the meeting.

How to learn phrasal verbs

  • Learn them with an object and a situation. Not just turn in, but turn in your report by Friday.
  • Group them by situation. At the office: set up a meeting, go over the plan. On the road: pick up, drop off, run out of gas. Health: throw up, get over a cold.
  • Notice the small word. Patterns help, even if they are not rules. Up often means "completely": fill up the tank. Off often means "stop": turn off the engine, call off the trip.
  • Learn where the object goes. A pronoun often goes in the middle: turn the radio off or turn it off, but never turn off it.

The phrasal verbs lesson gives you common phrasal verbs with meanings and examples, plus flashcards and a quiz. Learn five a day, not fifty.

Idioms

An idiom is an expression you can't understand from its words alone. I'm a little under the weather means "I feel a little sick." Let's call it a day means "let's stop working for today." Are we on the same page? means "do we understand this the same way?"

Learn the idioms you hear in real conversations first, and note who says each one and when. The American idioms list gives each idiom a meaning and an example, with flashcards and a quiz.

Spaced review: meet each word again at the right time

If you never see a new word again, you lose it in a few days. If you review it every day, you waste time. The answer is spaced review: you meet the word again after longer and longer breaks, each one just before you would forget.

WhenWhat you do
Day 1Learn the word in a sentence and say it aloud. Test yourself once more before bed.
Day 3Read the sentence with the gap. Say the word before you turn the card.
Day 7Go the hard way: look at the meaning, then say the English word and a sentence.
Day 21Use the word in a new sentence of your own, out loud or in writing.
Day 45Final check. If you still know it quickly, move it to your "known" pile.

The rule is simple. If you know the word quickly, it moves to the next step. If you hesitate or get it wrong, it goes back to Day 1. Hard words come back often, and easy words leave you alone.

You don't need an app. A box of cards with five sections works, or a notebook with the next review date next to each word. For practice questions, the mistake box in the free trainer does this for you: it brings back the questions you missed after 1, 3 and 7 days.

Reviews add up quickly, so keep new words to five to eight a day.

Active recall: test yourself and say it out loud

Reading your cards again feels like studying, but your brain is only recognizing the words. To remember a word, you have to pull it out of your memory. This is active recall, and it is the most important habit in this guide.

  • Cover and recall. Cover the back of the card, read the gap sentence, and say the missing word before you look.
  • Work in both directions. English to meaning is easy. Meaning to English is harder, and it is the direction you need when you speak.
  • Explain it in English. Describe the word without your own language: A receipt is the piece of paper you get after you pay.

Say every word aloud

Both tests include listening, and a word you know only from the page can sound strange at normal speed. Saying each word aloud connects its sound to its meaning. Watch the stress, because it can change the word:

  • a REC-ord (noun): Keep a record of your hours. To re-CORD (verb): Please record the meeting.
  • a PRES-ent (a gift) and to pre-SENT (verb): She will present the plan at 1000.
  • desert, said DEZ-ert (a dry area), and dessert, said dih-ZURT (something sweet after dinner).

Learn the American sound, too: schedule is said SKED-jool, and lieutenant is said loo-TEN-ent. In the trainer's listening questions, press Play, then Slower, then "Show the text" to find any word you missed. Then repeat the sentence after the speaker.

A 15-minute daily routine

Fifteen minutes every day beats two hours on Sunday, because short daily sessions give you more reviews. Do it on a break, on the bus or before lights out.

  1. Minutes 1 to 5: review. Go through the cards that are due today. Say each answer aloud before you turn the card, then move the card on or back to Day 1.
  2. Minutes 6 to 10: learn five to eight new words from one theme, for example health. For each word, read an example, write your own sentence, mark the stress and add one partner or family member.
  3. Minutes 11 to 13: practice. Answer a few questions on the ECL vocabulary practice page or in the trainer. Every word you miss becomes a new card.
  4. Minutes 14 and 15: recall. Close everything and say today's new words from memory, with one sentence each. Put the ones you forgot at the front of tomorrow's pile.

Example: on Tuesday you learn prescription, pharmacy, symptom, sprain and fever. Your sentences might be I picked up my prescription at the pharmacy on base and I sprained my ankle during PT. On Thursday, Day 3 for these words, they come back first.

A four-week plan

Use the daily routine five or six days a week. Each week has a focus, but you keep reviewing the words from earlier weeks.

WeekFocusEvery dayEnd of the week
1Everyday and base-life words: time, places, health, shoppingFive to eight new words in sentences. Set up your cards and review dates.Test yourself on all Week 1 cards. Put every miss back on Day 1.
2Collocations and word familiesLearn each new word with one partner (make a reservation) and one family member (reserve).Write five true sentences about your week, each with a collocation.
3Phrasal verbs, idioms and confusable pairsFive phrasal verbs or two idioms, plus one confusable pair.Answer a set of vocabulary questions and list every word you missed.
4Mixed review and test conditionsOnly three to five new words. More review, and more listening with Play and Slower.Take a timed test in the trainer and turn every missed word into a card.

If you are also working on grammar and listening, combine this with the four-week ECL study plan and use these 15 minutes as its vocabulary part. After four weeks, keep the reviews going and start new themes.

How to measure your progress

You can't see words grow, so count them. Once a week, take five minutes to check these points:

  • Words that pass Day 21. A word you still know after three weeks is a word you own. This number matters more than the number of words you have seen.
  • Your weekly score. Answer the same kind of vocabulary set at the end of each week and write down the score. Compare week to week, not day to day.
  • Your speed. Take 20 old cards at random and go from meaning to English. How many can you say within three seconds?
  • Your ear. Notice how often you need "Show the text" in listening practice. Less often means your ear is catching up.
  • Your speaking. Describe your day with five words from this week. Record it on your phone and listen again a month later.

Keep a log, one line a week. For example: Week 3: 30 new words, 22 past Day 21, vocabulary set 15 out of 20 (Week 2: 12). If the numbers rise slowly, the method is working. If they stop rising, check the list below.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Learning long alphabetical lists of rare words. Abandon, abbreviation and ability have nothing in common, and a word like ubiquitous can wait. Learn frequent words by theme and situation.
  • Reading instead of testing. If you only read your cards, you feel ready but forget fast. Always try to recall first.
  • Learning a word without its partners. Responsible is only half a word without for, and depend needs on.
  • Too many new words at once. Forty words on Monday and none on Tuesday leads to a mountain of reviews, and then to quitting. Five to eight a day works better.
  • Studying in silence. If you never say a word, you may not recognize it in a recording.
  • Memorizing answers instead of words. Remembering that the answer to question 12 is B won't help on a new test. When you miss a question, learn the word, its sentence and the reason.
  • Translating word for word. Your language and English don't match one to one. Learn what the word means in its sentence, not just its translation.
