Simple past practice: -ed, irregular verbs, did and used to

Yesterday, ago, last week: when the time is finished, English uses the simple past. Learn the spelling and sound rules, the did pattern and used to, then try 15 new questions.

Updated October 18, 2026

The simple past looks easy, and that is exactly why it costs points. Most learners know that yesterday needs a past verb. The errors come from the details: didn't went instead of didn't go, stoped instead of stopped, they was instead of they were, or have bought with ago. On a multiple-choice test like the ECL or the ALCPT, the wrong choices are built from exactly these details, so a quick look is not enough.

The simple past matters in listening too. The -ed ending is short and quiet (worked sounds like "workt"), so you often have to rely on a time word such as last night or two days ago to know the tense.

Below you will find the rules on one screen: spelling, the three sounds of -ed, the irregular verbs you need most, did in questions and negatives, was and were, used to, and when to choose the present perfect instead. Then try 15 new questions with full explanations. For all the tenses in one place, read the verb tenses lesson, and check any form in the irregular verbs table.

The rules in brief

Spelling of regular verbs

The verb ends inRuleExamples

Don't double when the last syllable is not stressed (visit → visited, open → opened) or after w, x or y (show → showed, fix → fixed). American English keeps one l in traveled and canceled.

Questions and negatives with did

Did carries the past, so the main verb goes back to the base form: Did you call the clinic? I didn't go to PT this morning. Never mark the past twice: not didn't went, not Did you saw...? When who or what is the subject, there is no did: Who called you? More on this in the questions lesson.

Was and were

I, he, she, it + was; you, we, they + were. Be makes its own questions and negatives, without did: Were you at the briefing? The gym wasn't open.

Finished-time words

Yesterday, last night, two days ago, in 2019, on Monday, when I was a child and questions with when take the simple past: When did you arrive?

Used to for past habits

Used to + base form is for habits and states that are no longer true: I used to smoke, but I quit two years ago. After did, drop the d: Did you use to live off base? Don't confuse it with be used to + -ing, "be familiar with": I'm used to getting up at five.

Simple past or present perfect?

Simple past for a finished time: I lost my wallet last week. Present perfect for a period up to now: We have lived here since May. Practice the choice on the verb tenses practice page.

Common mistakes

  • Not: I didn't went to the gym yesterday. Say: I didn't go to the gym yesterday. After didn't, use the base form, because did already shows the past.
  • Not: Who did call you last night? Say: Who called you last night? When who is the subject, the verb takes its past form directly, with no did.
  • Not: I have bought these boots two weeks ago. Say: I bought these boots two weeks ago. Ago marks a finished time, so it always takes the simple past.
  • Not: We stoped for coffee on the way to the airport. Say: We stopped for coffee on the way to the airport. Stop ends in one vowel + one consonant, so the p doubles before -ed.
  • Not: Last month we buyed a used car. Say: Last month we bought a used car. Buy is irregular (buy, bought, bought), so it never takes -ed.
  • Not: They was late for formation. Say: They were late for formation. Were goes with you, we and they; was is only for I, he, she and it.
  • Not: I use to play soccer in high school. Say: I used to play soccer in high school. In statements, used to keeps the d, even though you can hardly hear it.

Practice questions

Choose an answer for each sentence, then open Show the answer to check it and read why the other choices are wrong.

  1. Sorry I ______ your call last night. My phone was in the car.

    • Adidn't answer
    • Bdidn't answered
    • Cdon't answer
    • Dhaven't answered
    Show the answer

    A. didn't answer

    Last night is a finished time, and in negatives did carries the past, so the main verb stays in the base form: didn't answer. Didn't answered is the classic mistake. It marks the past twice, once in did and again with -ed.

  2. I forgot my lunch at home, so I ______ a sandwich at the snack bar.

    • Abrought
    • Bbought
    • Cbuyed
    • Dhave bought
    Show the answer

    B. bought

    Buy is irregular: buy, bought, bought. Brought is the trap. It is the past of bring, which means carry something with you, and you can't bring a lunch you forgot. Buyed is never correct, because buy does not take -ed.

  3. We ______ to leave at six, but the car wouldn't start.

    • Aplaned
    • Bplanned
    • Chave planned
    • Dplan
    Show the answer

    B. planned

    Plan has one syllable and ends in one vowel + one consonant, so the n doubles before -ed: planned. Planed is a real word (to plane wood is to make it smooth), so a spell checker may not catch it. Have planned does not fit a finished story like this one.

  4. The instructions on the form ______ clear, so half of us filled out the wrong boxes.

    • Aweren't
    • Bwasn't
    • Cdidn't
    • Dhaven't been
    Show the answer

    A. weren't

    The subject is instructions, which is plural, so the verb is weren't. Wasn't is tempting because the singular noun form comes right before the blank, but the verb agrees with its subject, not with the nearest noun. Didn't is wrong because be never takes did.

  5. ______ the bus or drive to the training center yesterday?

    • ADid you took
    • BHave you taken
    • CDid you take
    • DDo you take
    Show the answer

    C. Did you take

    In a past question, did carries the tense, so both main verbs stay in the base form: Did you take the bus or drive? Did you took is wrong for the same reason as didn't went. The past is shown only once, in did.

  6. Only one of these past forms has an extra syllable when you say it: ______.

    • Aasked
    • Bplayed
    • Cfixed
    • Ddecided
    Show the answer

    D. decided

    The -ed ending adds a syllable, /id/, only after a t or d sound. Decide ends in a d sound, so decided gets an extra syllable. Asked is tempting because it is hard to say, but it has one syllable: /askt/. Played ends in /d/, fixed in /t/.

  7. My son ______ off his bike two days ago and hurt his knee.

    • Afelt
    • Bfalled
    • Chas fallen
    • Dfell
    Show the answer

    D. fell

    Fall is irregular: fall, fell, fallen. Felt is the tempting choice, but it is the past of feel (I felt sick). Has fallen cannot go with two days ago, a finished time. Notice hurt: its past form is the same as the base form.

  8. My family moved to Colorado ______ year, and the kids already love the snow.

    • Athe last
    • Bin last
    • Clast
    • Don last
    Show the answer

    C. last

    Last year, last week and last night take no preposition and no article: we moved here last year. In last year is a common mistake. The last year means the twelve months up to now, and it goes with the present perfect: We have saved a lot in the last year.

  1. When I was a kid, I ______ every summer on my uncle's farm in Iowa.

    • Ause to spend
    • Bused to spending
    • Cused to spend
    • Dwas used to spend
    Show the answer

    C. used to spend

    Used to + base form describes a past habit that is no longer true: I used to spend. Use to spend is a common spelling mistake, because the d is hard to hear. Used to spending needs be or get in front of it, and then it means be familiar with something.

  2. Who ______ the lights on in the conference room last night?

    • Aleft
    • Bdid leave
    • Cdid left
    • Dhas left
    Show the answer

    A. left

    When who is the subject of the question, there is no did: the verb simply takes its past form, Who left the lights on? Did leave is tempting because most past questions use did, but in a normal question you need did only when who is the object: Who did you see?

  3. Jake and I ______ each other since basic training, so I trust him completely.

    • Aknew
    • Bknow
    • Chave known
    • Dhad known
    Show the answer

    C. have known

    Since basic training describes a friendship that started in the past and continues now (I trust him), so use the present perfect: have known. Knew is tempting because basic training is in the past, but the simple past would suggest the friendship is over. Know cannot go with since.

  4. On the first day of the course last month, our instructor ______ us a simple trick for remembering irregular verbs.

    • Athought
    • Btaught
    • Cteached
    • Dhas taught
    Show the answer

    B. taught

    Teach is irregular: teach, taught, taught. Thought is the trap. It sounds similar, but it is the past of think. Teached is never correct, and has taught cannot go with a finished time like last month.

  5. When ______ in Germany for the language course?

    • Awas you
    • Bhave you been
    • Cdid you be
    • Dwere you
    Show the answer

    D. were you

    A question with when asks for a finished time, so it takes the simple past, and be makes its own questions without did: When were you there? Have you been is tempting, but the present perfect is not used in questions that start with when.

  6. I hated the cold at first, but after two winters in Alaska, I ______ it.

    • Agot used to
    • Bused to
    • Cuse to
    • Dwas used
    Show the answer

    A. got used to

    Get used to + a noun or -ing means become familiar with something, so after two winters you got used to it. Used to on its own must be followed by a verb and describes an old habit: I used to hate the cold. It cannot take a noun like it.

  7. A: Have you ever been to Japan? B: Yes, twice. I ______ there in 2018 and again last spring.

    • Ahave gone
    • Bwent
    • Chave been
    • Dgo
    Show the answer

    B. went

    The question with ever asks about experience, so it uses the present perfect. The answer gives finished times (in 2018, last spring), so it switches to the simple past: I went. Have been is tempting because the question uses it, but it cannot go with in 2018.

Tips for the test

  • See did, think base form. Any choice with did or didn't + a past form (didn't went, did you saw) is wrong, so cross it out first.
  • Let the time word decide. Ago, yesterday, last week, in 2019 and questions with when rule out have/has + past participle. Since and for up to now point the other way.
  • Find the real subject for was/were. In The keys to the supply room ______ on the desk, the subject is keys, so the verb is were.
  • Watch for look-alike pairs. Fell (fall) and felt (feel), thought (think) and taught (teach), bought (buy) and brought (bring) are easy to mix up.
  • In listening, catch the time word. The -ed ending is easy to miss, but last night or an hour ago tells you the tense before the verb does.
  • Review a few verbs every day. Learn ten forms from the irregular verbs table, then use them in the free trainer, where missed questions come back after 1, 3 and 7 days.

Questions learners ask

How can I tell if a verb is regular or irregular?

You can't tell from the base form, so the common irregular verbs have to be learned. The good news is that the list you need every day is short: be, have, do, go, get, make, take, come, see, say, give and a few dozen more. Learn them in groups that change the same way (think, thought; bring, brought) and check the rest in the irregular verbs table. New verbs, like text or email, are regular: I texted her, I emailed the office.

Is it "Did you use to" or "Did you used to"?

After did, the standard spelling is use to, without the d: Did you use to live off base? We didn't use to have a gym on this base. In statements, keep the d: I used to live off base. In speech the two sound the same, so this matters mainly when you read or write, for example in a grammar question.

Why does "worked" sound like "workt" when people speak?

The -ed ending is said /t/ after sounds made without the voice, such as k, p, s, sh, ch and f. So worked, stopped and watched each have one syllable and end in a quick t sound. Only verbs ending in a t or d sound add a syllable: wanted, needed. When you listen, don't wait for a clear -ed. Listen for the time word (last night, two days ago) and for did in questions.
