Phrasal verbs practice: separable verbs, particles and traps

Turn it off or turn off it? Look after them or look them after? Learn the word order rules, what the small words add, and the pairs learners confuse, then test yourself on 15 new questions.

Updated October 18, 2026

A phrasal verb is a verb plus one or two small words, such as up, off, out or into. The small word is called a particle, and it changes the meaning of the verb. You know call and you know off, but together call off means "cancel." Very often, you cannot guess the meaning from the parts.

Phrasal verbs cost points in two ways. First, the choices often keep the same verb and change only the particle: put off, put out, put up, put on. If you are not sure of each meaning, all four look possible. Second, word order: you can say call the meeting off or call it off, but never call off it. The ECL and the ALCPT are multiple-choice tests of listening and reading in American English, and Americans use phrasal verbs all the time when they talk, so you need them for listening as much as for reading.

This page explains the three word order types, what the most common particles add to a verb, three-part verbs like put up with, and the pairs that learners mix up. Then you can try 15 new questions, each with an explanation. To build your vocabulary, study the 309 phrasal verbs with meanings, examples and flashcards.

How phrasal verbs work

Three word order types

Each meaning of a phrasal verb belongs to one of three types. The type tells you where the object goes.

TypeWith a nounWith a pronoun
Separableturn off the heater or turn the heater offturn it off (never turn off it)
Inseparablelook after my sonlook after him (never look him after)
No objectMy car broke down on the highway.Nothing follows the verb.
  • With a separable verb, a pronoun (it, them, me, her) must go in the middle: Your jacket is on the floor. Please hang it up.
  • A long object usually goes after the particle: Fill out the form on the back of the card.
  • Some verbs have two types, with two meanings: The plane took off (no object: it left the ground), but Take off your shoes (separable: remove).

What the particle adds

ParticleCommon meaningExamples
upcompletely, until nothing is left; also more or higheruse up, eat up, clean up, turn up the volume
offstop, cancel, or away from a placeturn off, call off, wear off, set off
outto the end, completely; also outsidesell out, run out, figure out, wear out
downless, lower, or onto paperslow down, turn down, write down
backreturncall back, give back, pay back
oncontinuekeep on, carry on, go on

These meanings are clues, not laws. The tickets sold out means none were left, but when an alarm goes off, it starts ringing.

Three-part verbs

Some verbs have two particles. They are inseparable, so the object goes at the end: We ran out of milk. My daughter came up with a name for the puppy. I'm looking forward to the weekend. The to in look forward to is a preposition, so a verb after it takes -ing: We're looking forward to moving.

Same verb, different particle

VerbMeaningExample
put offpostponeWe put off the party until May.
put outstop a fire from burningPut out the campfire before you leave.
put upbuild or hang; let someone stayWe put up a tent. A friend put me up after my flight was canceled.
put up withtolerateMy wife can't put up with my snoring.
put ondress inPut on a warm jacket.

Common mistakes

  • Not: I can't hear the radio. Can you turn up it? Say: Can you turn it up? With a separable verb, a pronoun always goes between the verb and the particle.
  • Not: My mother will look them after this weekend. Say: My mother will look after them this weekend. Look after is inseparable, so the object comes after the particle, even when it is a pronoun.
  • Not: He was grown up in a small town in Texas. Say: He grew up in a small town in Texas. Grow up has no object, so it has no passive form.
  • Not: I look forward to work with you. Say: I look forward to working with you. In look forward to, to is a preposition, so the verb after it takes -ing.
  • Not: She gave up to smoke last year. Say: She gave up smoking last year. Give up means stop, and like stop, it is followed by an -ing form (see gerunds and infinitives).
  • Not: I'll return back your charger tomorrow. Say: I'll give your charger back tomorrow. Return already means give back, so return back says the same thing twice.
  • Not: Please fill this form before your appointment. Say: Please fill out this form before your appointment. In American English, you fill out a form, while fill alone means put something into a container until it is full.

Practice questions

Choose an answer for each sentence, then open Show the answer to check it and read why the other choices are wrong.

  1. Hurry up! We're running ______ time, and the bus leaves in five minutes.

    • Aout of
    • Bout
    • Coff
    • Daway
    Show the answer

    A. out of

    Run out of means have none left, and when the thing that is finished comes after the verb, you need of: running out of time. Out alone is tempting, but run out takes no object, as in Our time is running out.

  2. Your boots are covered in mud. Please ______ before you come into the kitchen.

    • Atake off them
    • Btake them off
    • Ctake it off
    • Dtake them on
    Show the answer

    B. take them off

    Take off means remove clothes or shoes, and it is separable. With a pronoun, the pronoun must go in the middle: take them off. Take off them is the most common mistake; the order take off + object works only with a noun, as in take off your boots.

  3. I thought I had lost my car keys, but I ______ in my gym bag this morning.

    • Acame them across
    • Bcame across it
    • Ccame across them
    • Dcame them over
    Show the answer

    C. came across them

    Come across means find by chance, and it is inseparable, so even a pronoun goes after the particle: came across them. Came them across copies the order of separable verbs like pick them up, which does not work here. Came across it is wrong because keys is plural.

  4. The heater ______ in the middle of the night, so we all woke up freezing.

    • Abroke out
    • Bbroke up
    • Cbroke down
    • Dbroke into
    Show the answer

    C. broke down

    Break down means stop working, and it takes no object: The heater broke down. Broke out is the tempting choice, but break out means start suddenly, like a fire or a fight, or escape. Broke into needs an object, as in Someone broke into the garage.

  5. We had to put off our trip to Florida until the spring because of my knee surgery.

    • Acancel
    • Bpostpone
    • Cplan
    • Dpay for
    Show the answer

    B. postpone

    Put off means postpone: the trip will still happen, only later. Cancel is the tempting choice, but that is the meaning of call off, and something you call off does not happen at all. The words until the spring show that this is a delay.

  6. Before you return the rental car, make sure you fill ______ the tank.

    • Aout
    • Bin
    • Con
    • Dup
    Show the answer

    D. up

    Fill up means fill completely, and up often adds the idea of completely: fill up, use up, clean up. Fill out is the tempting choice because it is so common, but you fill out a form, not a gas tank.

  7. The concert tickets sold ______ in less than an hour, so we watched the show online.

    • Aoff
    • Bup
    • Caway
    • Dout
    Show the answer

    D. out

    Sell out means sell everything until none is left; here out means to the end. Sold off is tempting, but sell off means sell things cheaply to get rid of them, which is not what happens when fans buy every ticket.

  8. My second cup of coffee has already worn ______, and I can't stop yawning.

    • Aoff
    • Bout
    • Cdown
    • Daway
    Show the answer

    A. off

    Wear off means slowly stop having an effect, and off carries the idea of stopping. Worn out is the tempting choice, but wear out means use something until it breaks, or make a person very tired. The coffee is not the one who is tired.

  1. The whole family is looking forward ______ Grandpa at Thanksgiving.

    • Ato see
    • Bseeing
    • Cto seeing
    • Dfor seeing
    Show the answer

    C. to seeing

    Look forward to is a three-part verb, and its to is a preposition, so the verb after it takes -ing: looking forward to seeing Grandpa. To see is the most common mistake, because learners think this to is part of an infinitive.

  2. How do you ______ all that noise from the construction site next door?

    • Aput up with
    • Bput up
    • Cput off
    • Dput down
    Show the answer

    A. put up with

    Put up with means tolerate, and it needs all three parts: put up with the noise. Put up alone is tempting, but it means build, hang or give someone a place to stay. Put off means postpone, which makes no sense with noise.

  3. The candles are still burning. Could you ______ before we go to bed?

    • Aput out them
    • Bput them off
    • Cput them up
    • Dput them out
    Show the answer

    D. put them out

    Put out means stop something from burning, and because it is separable, the pronoun goes in the middle: put them out. Put out them has the right verb but the wrong word order. Put them off would mean postpone them, and put them up would mean hang them.

  4. Our old neighbors offered to put us up while our new house was being painted.

    • Adrive us around
    • Blend us money
    • Ctolerate us
    • Dgive us a place to stay
    Show the answer

    D. give us a place to stay

    Put someone up means let someone sleep at your home for a short time. Tolerate us is the trap, because that meaning needs three parts: put up with us. The sentence is about where the family stayed while the painters worked.

  5. Don't worry about the problem with the schedule; we'll work it out tomorrow morning.

    • Aexercise
    • Bfind a solution
    • Ccancel it
    • Dwrite it down
    Show the answer

    B. find a solution

    Work something out means find a solution to a problem. Exercise is the tempting choice, because work out also means exercise, but that meaning takes no object: I work out at the gym. Here the object it, the problem, shows the solving meaning.

  6. My alarm didn't ______ this morning, so I was late for formation.

    • Ago out
    • Bgo away
    • Cgo off
    • Dgo by
    Show the answer

    C. go off

    When an alarm goes off, it starts ringing. This surprises many learners, because off usually means stop, as in turn off. Go out is the tempting choice, but lights and fires go out, which means they stop.

  7. Only six people ______ for the Saturday morning run, but we went anyway.

    • Ashowed up
    • Bshowed off
    • Cshowed out
    • Dshowed in
    Show the answer

    A. showed up

    Show up means arrive or come to an event, and it takes no object: Only six people showed up. Showed off looks similar, but show off means try to impress people with your skills or things, so it does not fit a sentence about who came.

Tips for the test

  • Read past the blank. The words after the verb often decide the particle: the meeting ... until Friday points to put off, and the fire points to put out.
  • Same words in a different order? Look at the object. A pronoun goes in the middle of a separable verb (hang it up) but after an inseparable or three-part verb (look after it, run out of it).
  • Use the particle as a clue. Up often means completely, off stop or away, out to the end, and back return. This can help you remove one or two choices.
  • For a starred verb, test each meaning in the sentence. Put each choice in place of the phrasal verb and read the sentence again. Watch out for a meaning that belongs to a different particle, such as "cancel" for put off.
  • Listen for the particle. In speech, the particle is usually stressed: turn it off, she showed up. It carries the meaning, so do not let it slip past you.
  • Learn verbs in families, each with a sentence. Study one verb with its particles (put off, put out, put up), use the flashcards on the phrasal verbs page, and review missed questions in the free trainer. For more everyday English, try the American idioms page.

Questions learners ask

How can I tell if a phrasal verb is separable?

Usually you cannot tell from the form, so learn each verb with one example sentence that shows where the object goes. Two hints help. Verbs with two particles, like run out of, are always inseparable. Verbs with no object, like break down, never take one. On the phrasal verbs lesson page, every verb is marked separable, inseparable or no object.

Are phrasal verbs too informal for writing?

Most are fine in any style: find out, set up, carry out and take over appear in reports and emails every day. A few are casual, such as hang out or mess up. In very formal writing, a one-word verb can sound better, like postpone for put off or tolerate for put up with. In conversation, phrasal verbs are the normal choice.

What is the best way to remember phrasal verbs?

Learn a few at a time, each with a short sentence about your own life, such as "I need to fill out my leave form." Group them by verb (put off, put out, put up) or by topic (travel, health, work). Say the sentences aloud, test yourself with flashcards, and come back to the ones you miss after a few days.

Is "look at" a phrasal verb?

Not in the usual sense. In look at, the word at keeps its normal meaning, and the object always comes after it. In a phrasal verb like look up a word, the meaning changes: you search for the word in a dictionary. On the test, the name matters less than two questions: what does the whole verb mean, and where does the object go?
