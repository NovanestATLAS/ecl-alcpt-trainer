Conditionals practice: 15 questions on if, unless and wish

Fifteen new questions on every kind of if sentence, from simple facts to formal inversions. Each explanation shows why the right answer works and why the tempting one does not.

Updated October 18, 2026

A conditional sentence has two parts: a condition and a result. If the bus is late, we'll take a taxi. The verb forms in each part tell your listener whether you mean a fact, a real plan, something imaginary, or a past that did not happen. Change one verb and you change the meaning.

That is why conditionals cost points on the ECL and the ALCPT. The four choices often look almost the same: will go, would go, would have gone, had gone. Only one of them fits the time and the reality of the sentence. Some sentences hide the condition in a small word such as unless or in case, and formal ones such as Had I known have no if at all. In the listening part, the short form 'd can mean had or would, and you have to decide which one you heard.

On this page you get the rules in short tables, common learner mistakes, 15 new practice questions and tips for the test. Every explanation tells you why the right answer is right and why the most tempting wrong answer is wrong. If the topic is new to you, read the full lesson on conditionals first, then come back and test yourself.

The rules in brief

Ask two questions: Is it about now, the future or the past? Is it real or imaginary?

TypeIf clauseResult clauseUse and example
Zeropresent simplepresent simpleFacts and things that always happen: If you enter the wrong code, the door stays locked.
Firstpresent simplewill / can / may / might + verbA real possibility in the future: If the store is open, I'll buy some batteries.
Secondpast simple (were for be)would / could / might + verbImaginary now, or unlikely in the future: If I lived near the base, I would walk to work.
Thirdpast perfect (had + past participle)would / could / might have + past participleAn imaginary past, often a regret: If we had checked the forecast, we wouldn't have gone sailing.
Mixedpast perfectwould + verbA different past, so a different present: If I had kept my old job, I would live in Texas now.

A second mixed pattern works the other way: a present fact explains a past result. If I didn't speak Korean, I wouldn't have gotten this job. In all these patterns, will and would do not go in the if clause. Need a refresher on had + past participle? See the past perfect in verb tenses.

Other condition words

WordMeaningExample
unlessif ... notWe'll start without you unless you're here by eight.
as long as, provided (that)only ifYou can borrow my truck as long as you fill the tank. Provided that the roads are clear, we'll leave at six.
in caseas a precaution, because it might happenTake some cash in case the card reader is down.
even ifthe result does not changeI'm going for a run even if it snows.

Like if, these words take a present verb for the future: unless it rains, not unless it will rain.

Wish and if only

  • wish + past simple: you want the present to be different. I wish I had a bigger kitchen. Use were for be: I wish it were Friday.
  • wish + past perfect: a regret about the past. I wish I had saved that file.
  • wish + would: you want another person to change. I wish the neighbors would turn down their music. Don't use it about yourself.
  • wish + could: an ability you don't have. I wish I could stay longer.

If only takes the same forms but sounds stronger: If only I had listened!

Formal inversion: no if

In formal English you can drop if and put should, were or had before the subject.

  • Should + subject + base verb (first conditional): Should you need a ride, call the front desk.
  • Were + subject (+ to + verb) for the second conditional: Were I in your place, I would wait. Were we to lose power, the backup lights would come on.
  • Had + subject + past participle (third conditional): Had we known about the road work, we would have left earlier.

For the negative, put not after the subject: Had I not seen the sign, I would have missed the exit. For could have and might have, see modal verbs practice.

Common mistakes

  • Not: If it will snow tonight, the roads will be closed. Say: If it snows tonight, the roads will be closed. The if clause takes the present simple, even when you talk about the future.
  • Not: If I would have known about the party, I would have come. Say: If I had known about the party, I would have come. Would have belongs in the result only; the if clause takes the past perfect.
  • Not: If I had taken the earlier flight, I would have been home now. Say: If I had taken the earlier flight, I would be home now. The word now shows a present result, so you need a mixed conditional with would + verb.
  • Not: Unless you don't sign the form, you won't get paid. Say: Unless you sign the form, you won't get paid. Unless already means "if not," so a second not turns the meaning upside down.
  • Not: Bring an umbrella in case it will rain. Say: Bring an umbrella in case it rains. Like if, in case takes the present simple for a future event.
  • Not: I wish I can drive. Say: I wish I could drive. After wish, move the verb one step back into the past to show that the situation is not real.
  • Not: Hadn't we stopped for gas, we would have run out of fuel on the highway. Say: Had we not stopped for gas, we would have run out of fuel on the highway. In an inverted conditional, not comes after the subject and is never contracted.

Practice questions

Choose an answer for each sentence, then open Show the answer to check it and read why the other choices are wrong.

  1. If you ______ the wrong password three times, the system automatically locks you out for an hour.

    • Aenter
    • Bwill enter
    • Centered
    • Dwould enter
    Show the answer

    A. enter

    This describes how the system always works, so it is a zero conditional: present simple in both parts (enter ... locks). Will enter is tempting because the event is in the future, but will does not go in the if clause.

  2. If the package doesn't arrive by Friday, I ______ the store and ask for a refund.

    • Awould call
    • Bcalled
    • Cwill call
    • Dwould have called
    Show the answer

    C. will call

    The condition is real and possible, and it is in the present simple (doesn't arrive), so this is a first conditional with will + verb. Would call is tempting, but it needs a past verb in the if clause (If it didn't arrive), and would have called is only for the past.

  3. If I ______ fix computers myself, I wouldn't have to pay a technician every time this laptop crashes.

    • Acan
    • Bcould
    • Cwould
    • Dwill be able to
    Show the answer

    B. could

    Wouldn't have to shows a second conditional: an imaginary present, because the speaker can't fix computers. The if clause needs a past form, and could is the past of can. Can is tempting, but a present verb does not match wouldn't. Would never goes after if in this pattern.

  4. If the hotel ______ us about the pool repairs, we would have booked a different place.

    • Atold
    • Bhas told
    • Cwould have told
    • Dhad told
    Show the answer

    D. had told

    Would have booked shows an imaginary past: the hotel did not tell us, and we did not book another place. A third conditional needs the past perfect (had told) after if. Would have told is the classic mistake: would have goes only in the result, never in the if clause.

  5. If you had mentioned the job opening last month, I ______ for it before the deadline.

    • Acould apply
    • Bcould have applied
    • Ccan apply
    • Dhad applied
    Show the answer

    B. could have applied

    Had mentioned and last month put the whole situation in the past, and it did not happen, so the result needs could have + past participle. Could apply is tempting, but it talks about now or the future, and the deadline has already passed.

  6. If I hadn't spent so much on car repairs last month, I ______ enough money for the trip now.

    • Awould have
    • Bwould have had
    • Cwill have
    • Dhad had
    Show the answer

    A. would have

    The condition is in the past (last month), but the result is about now. This mixed conditional uses would + verb in the result: I would have (own) enough money now. Would have had is the tempting third-conditional form, but it does not fit the word now.

  7. Mark ______ on the wrong bus yesterday if he weren't always in such a hurry.

    • Awouldn't get
    • Bwon't get
    • Chadn't gotten
    • Dwouldn't have gotten
    Show the answer

    D. wouldn't have gotten

    If he weren't always in such a hurry describes Mark in general, now and always. The result happened yesterday, so it needs wouldn't have + past participle. Wouldn't get is tempting because weren't looks like a normal second conditional, but yesterday points to the past.

  8. You won't get a parking permit unless you ______ proof of insurance.

    • Adon't show
    • Bwill show
    • Cshow
    • Dshowed
    Show the answer

    C. show

    Unless means if not: you won't get a permit if you don't show proof. After unless, use the present simple for the future, so show is correct. Don't show is the trap: with unless it makes a double negative and turns the meaning upside down.

  1. Pack a few snacks ______ your flight is delayed and the airport restaurants are closed.

    • Ain case
    • Bunless
    • Cprovided that
    • Deven if
    Show the answer

    A. in case

    You pack the snacks now, as a precaution, because a delay might happen. That is the meaning of in case. Provided that is tempting because a condition follows it too, but it means only if, and you cannot wait to see whether the flight is delayed before you pack.

  2. You can borrow my camera as long as you bring it back by Sunday.

    • Aeven though
    • Bonly if
    • Cunless
    • Dbecause
    Show the answer

    B. only if

    Here as long as sets a condition: you can borrow the camera only if you return it by Sunday. In this sentence it does not talk about time. Unless is tempting because it is also a condition word, but it means if not, the opposite. Even though shows contrast.

  3. I wish I ______ Spanish. Half of my new coworkers speak it at lunch, and I can't join in.

    • Aspeak
    • Bwould speak
    • Cspoke
    • Dhad spoken
    Show the answer

    C. spoke

    The wish is about the present (I can't join in now), so wish takes the past simple: I wish I spoke. Would speak is tempting, but wish + would is for changes you want from other people, not for your own situation. Had spoken would be a regret about the past.

  4. If only I ______ the extra insurance when I rented that car last weekend!

    • Abought
    • Bhave bought
    • Cwould buy
    • Dhad bought
    Show the answer

    D. had bought

    This is a regret about the past, so if only takes the past perfect: had bought. Bought is tempting because of last weekend, but after wish and if only you move one step further back in time, and the past simple becomes the past perfect.

  5. Should your flight ______ canceled, go straight to the service desk for a new ticket.

    • Ais
    • Bbeen
    • Cbe
    • Dwill be
    Show the answer

    C. be

    Should your flight be canceled is a formal way to say If your flight is canceled. In this inversion, should is followed by the base form of the verb, so be is correct. Is is tempting because it is the normal form after if, but it cannot follow should.

  6. Had I ______ the traffic would be so bad, I would have taken the train.

    • Aknown
    • Bknew
    • Chave known
    • Dknow
    Show the answer

    A. known

    Had I known means If I had known: a third conditional with had + past participle, and the past participle of know is known. Knew is tempting because the sentence is about the past, but after had you always need the past participle (had gone, had seen, had known).

  7. ______ we to miss the last bus, we would have to take a taxi back to the base.

    • AShould
    • BWere
    • CWould
    • DDid
    Show the answer

    B. Were

    Were + subject + to + verb is a formal second conditional for an unlikely future: Were we to miss = If we missed. Should is tempting because it also begins formal conditionals, but it is followed by the base form (Should we miss), never by to.

Tips for the test

  • Find the time words first. Now, today and these days point to would + verb; yesterday and last year point to would have + past participle. If the two halves talk about different times, expect a mixed conditional.
  • Match the pairs. Present goes with will, past simple with would, past perfect with would have. When one half is given, the other half usually follows from it.
  • Cross out will and would after if. In a normal condition they are wrong after if, unless, in case and as long as, so you can often remove two choices at once.
  • Test unless with if not. Put if ... not in its place and read the sentence again. If the meaning becomes strange, the choice is wrong.
  • Spot the hidden if. A sentence that starts with Should, Were or Had and has a comma in the middle is usually a conditional: Should + base verb, Were + to + verb, Had + past participle.
  • Review what you miss. The conditionals set in the free trainer explains every answer, and the trainer's mistake box brings back missed questions after 1, 3 and 7 days. For mixed grammar, try ECL grammar practice.

Questions learners ask

Is "If I was you" wrong?

In relaxed American speech you will often hear "If I was you." In writing and on the test, "If I were you" is the safe choice, because were is used for every person (I, he, she, it) in an imaginary if clause. When the situation is imaginary and both forms appear in the choices, choose were.

Can will or would ever come after if?

Yes, in two special cases. In polite requests, would means "please": If you would wait here, the doctor will see you soon. And will can mean "be willing to": If you'll carry the boxes, I'll hold the door. In a normal condition about a future event, though, will and would do not go after if, so on the test they are usually the wrong choice.

When I hear 'd in a conditional, is it had or would?

Look at the next verb. 'd + past participle (I'd known, we'd left) is had. 'd + base verb (I'd go, she'd know) is would, and 'd have + past participle is would have. In "If I'd known, I'd have called you," the first 'd is had and the second is would.

What is the difference between in case and if?

If makes your action depend on the event: I'll call the plumber if the pipe leaks again, so you call only after it leaks. In case means you act now, as a precaution: I'll buy a spare part in case the pipe leaks again, so you buy it today, whether it leaks or not.
