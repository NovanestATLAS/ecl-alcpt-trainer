Prepositions practice: time, place and fixed combinations

Prepositions follow habits more than logic. Learn the patterns for time, place, movement and fixed combinations like responsible for, then test yourself on 15 new questions.

Updated October 18, 2026

Prepositions are the short words that connect a noun to the rest of a sentence: at 0800, on Monday, into the truck, responsible for the keys. They look easy, but they cause mistakes at every level, because the right one often depends on habit, not logic. Your own language may use one word where English uses three, or a completely different word.

The ECL and the ALCPT are multiple-choice tests of listening and reading in American English, and preposition items are hard to guess. All the choices are short, they all look possible, and only one sounds right to a native speaker. In the listening part, one preposition can change the whole message: "Finish it by Friday" and "Work on it until Friday" ask for different things.

This page gives you the rules in brief, with tables, the mistakes learners make most often, 15 new questions with explanations, and tips for test day. For the full picture, read the prepositions lesson. When you want more practice, the free trainer has 4,309 explained questions and brings back the ones you miss.

The rules in brief

Time: at, on, in

WordUse it withExample
atclock times and exact moments: at 0800, at noon, at nightThe first bus to town leaves at 0545.
ondays and dates, also a named day plus a part of itHer promotion ceremony is on Friday morning.
inmonths, years, seasons, parts of the day; a time from nowMy lease ends in July. The doctor will see you in ten minutes.

Use no preposition before next, last, this, every, today and tomorrow: We fly home next Tuesday.

Time: by or until, for or during

  • by = a deadline, at that time or earlier: Please send me your slides by noon.
  • until = up to that time, for something that continues: The library is open until 2100. With not, it means "not before": The new printer won't arrive until Monday.
  • for + a length of time (how long?): We stayed in the field for nine days. Use since + a starting point: since June. Both usually go with the present perfect (see verb tenses).
  • during + a noun (when?): Nobody spoke during the test. Before a subject and a verb, use while: while we were eating.

Place and movement

WordMeaningExample
ata point, a building, an eventI'll wait for you at the main gate.
ininside a space; a city, state or countryHer family lives in Ohio.
ona surface, a floor, a streetThe duty roster is on the wall.
into / ontomovement to the inside / to the top of somethingHe got into the taxi. The singer jumped onto the stage.
acrossfrom one side to the other of a flat area or a lineWe walked across the parking lot.
throughinside something, from one end to the otherThe road goes through a forest.

Arrive takes at for a building or a point (arrive at the hospital) and in for a city or a country (arrive in Chicago), never to. With home, here and there, use no preposition: We arrived home late.

Fixed combinations

Learn these as one unit. If a verb follows, it takes -ing (see gerunds and infinitives).

  • responsible for: Who is responsible for the vehicle keys?
  • interested in: I'm interested in learning to fly.
  • accuse someone of: They accused him of lying.
  • depend on: Your leave dates depend on the training schedule.
  • apply for the thing you want, apply to the place: She applied for a job at the hospital.
  • in charge of: Who is in charge of the night shift?

American usage

  • on the weekend (British: at the weekend): We go fishing on the weekend.
  • in line (British: in a queue): Get in line behind the last person.
  • different from, or in speech different than: The new uniform is different from the old one.
  • Monday through Friday = Monday to Friday, with Friday included: The clinic is open Monday through Friday.
  • on a street, at an address: The bank is on Main Street, at number 25.

Common mistakes

  • Not: I need your answer until Friday. Say: I need your answer by Friday. Giving an answer happens once, so it needs the deadline word by; until is for actions that continue.
  • Not: Call me when you arrive to the hotel. Say: Call me when you arrive at the hotel. Arrive takes at or in, never to, even though the idea is movement.
  • Not: My phone rang during I was driving. Say: My phone rang while I was driving. During needs a noun after it (during the drive); before a subject and a verb, use while.
  • Not: See you on next Monday. Say: See you next Monday. Words like next, last, this and every already do the job of the preposition, so drop it.
  • Not: She is responsible of the new recruits. Say: She is responsible for the new recruits. English always says responsible for a person or a task, never responsible of.
  • Not: He was accused for stealing a car. Say: He was accused of stealing a car. Accuse takes of; for belongs to blame, as in blame someone for something.
  • Not: The road goes across a long tunnel. Say: The road goes through a long tunnel. A tunnel is all around you, so you go through it; across is for flat places like a field or a street.

Practice questions

Choose an answer for each sentence, then open Show the answer to check it and read why the other choices are wrong.

  1. Our driver training course begins ______ Monday ______ 0800, so don't plan anything for that morning.

    • Aon / at
    • Bin / on
    • Cat / in
    • Don / in
    Show the answer

    A. on / at

    Use on with days and dates (on Monday, on May 3) and at with clock times (at 0800). The tempting choice on / in is wrong because in goes with longer periods, such as months, years and seasons, never with a clock time.

  2. We just moved into a small house ______ Maple Avenue, two blocks from the school.

    • Aat
    • Bin
    • Con
    • Dto
    Show the answer

    C. on

    In American English, a house or a store is on a street: on Maple Avenue. Use at only with the full address, number included: at 412 Maple Avenue. In is for cities, states and countries; British speakers sometimes use it with streets, but American English does not.

  3. We have to return the rental van ______ 1800 on Sunday, or they will charge us for another day.

    • Auntil
    • Bby
    • Csince
    • Don
    Show the answer

    B. by

    By gives a deadline: the van must be back at 1800 or earlier. Until is the tempting choice, but it is for an action that continues up to a time, as in We can keep the van until Sunday. Returning the van happens once, so it needs by.

  4. When my parents arrived ______ the United States, they spoke almost no English.

    • Aat
    • Bto
    • Con
    • Din
    Show the answer

    D. in

    Use arrive in with cities and countries (arrive in the United States, arrive in Dallas) and arrive at with buildings and points, such as an airport or a gate. Arrive to is a very common mistake: arrive is never followed by the preposition to.

  5. The store manager accused the new cashier ______ taking money from the register.

    • Afor
    • Bwith
    • Cof
    • Dabout
    Show the answer

    C. of

    The pattern is accuse someone of doing something, with an -ing verb after of. For is tempting because we say blame someone for something, but accuse never takes for. With belongs to a different verb, charge: he was charged with theft.

  6. A storm knocked out the power, and we were without electricity ______ six hours.

    • Afor
    • Bduring
    • Csince
    • Dwhile
    Show the answer

    A. for

    For tells how long something lasts: for six hours, for two weeks. During answers the question when, and it needs a named event or period, such as during the storm, so during six hours is wrong. Since needs a starting point, such as since noon.

  7. He climbed ______ the roof of the garage to clear the leaves out of the gutter.

    • Ainto
    • Bonto
    • Cthrough
    • Dat
    Show the answer

    B. onto

    Onto shows movement to a position on top of a surface, so you climb onto a roof, a stage or a table. Into is the tempting choice, but it is for movement to the inside of a space, such as a room, a car or a box.

  8. Life on a ship is very different ______ life on a base, and many sailors need a few weeks to adjust.

    • Aof
    • Bwith
    • Cfor
    • Dfrom
    Show the answer

    D. from

    Different from is correct in every kind of English and is the safest choice on a test. Different of and different with are word-for-word translations from other languages, and English does not use them. In conversation, Americans also say different than.

  1. During the holidays, the base gym stays open ______ midnight, so you can work out after dinner.

    • Aby
    • Bsince
    • Cuntil
    • Dfor
    Show the answer

    C. until

    Until marks the end of something that continues: the gym is open all evening, up to midnight. By is the tempting choice, but it gives a deadline for one action that happens at that time or earlier, as in Be back by midnight.

  2. You should apply ______ a new passport at least two months before your trip.

    • Afor
    • Bto
    • Con
    • Dwith
    Show the answer

    A. for

    Apply for the thing you want: a passport, a job, a loan, a week of leave. Apply to the place or the people who decide: apply to a college, apply to the passport office. A passport is the thing you want, so for is right and to is wrong.

  3. We usually visit my wife's parents ______ the weekend, when the kids don't have school.

    • Ain
    • Bat
    • Cby
    • Don
    Show the answer

    D. on

    Americans say on the weekend, just as they say on Saturday. At the weekend is British English, and the ECL and ALCPT use American English, so choose on. In the weekend and by the weekend do not fit here.

  4. The exit was on the other side of the hall, so we had to push our way ______ the crowd.

    • Aacross
    • Bthrough
    • Calong
    • Donto
    Show the answer

    B. through

    Through is for movement inside something that surrounds you, from one side to the other: through a crowd, a tunnel or a forest. Across is the tempting choice, but it is for a flat surface or a line, such as across the street or across a field.

  5. Someone's phone rang twice ______ the graduation ceremony, and everyone turned around to look.

    • Aduring
    • Bwhile
    • Cfor
    • Dsince
    Show the answer

    A. during

    During is followed by a noun: during the ceremony, during lunch. While has the same meaning but must be followed by a subject and a verb: while the band was playing. For and since do not fit, because no length of time or starting point is given.

  6. The education center is open Monday through Friday. This means it is open:

    • Aon Monday and Friday only
    • Bevery day except Monday and Friday
    • Cfrom Monday to Friday, including Friday
    • Dfrom Monday until Thursday
    Show the answer

    C. from Monday to Friday, including Friday

    In American English, Monday through Friday means from Monday to Friday, with Friday included, and you will see it on signs and schedules. The tempting choice, until Thursday, is wrong: through always includes the last day named.

  7. Sergeant Kim is ______ charge of the training calendar, so the date of your test depends ______ her.

    • Aon / of
    • Bin / from
    • Cat / in
    • Din / on
    Show the answer

    D. in / on

    The fixed expressions are in charge of, meaning in control of, and depend on. In / from is tempting because the first part is right, but depend from and depend of are word-for-word translations from other languages and are wrong in English.

Tips for the test

  • Read past the blank. The word after it often decides: a clock time takes at, a day takes on, a month takes in, and a country after arrive takes in.
  • For time items, ask one question. Does it happen once before a deadline (by) or continue up to a point (until)? Does it answer "how long?" (for) or "when?" (during)?
  • Learn fixed combinations with an example from your own life, such as I'm in charge of the coffee fund. A pair you have used yourself is easier to remember under pressure.
  • Do not mix up prepositions and the small words in phrasal verbs: look for and look after are separate verbs with their own meanings.
  • In listening, prepositions are short and weak, so at, in and on can sound almost the same. Listen for the noun that follows, and use the Slower button in the trainer when you practice.
  • Review your misses. The trainer's mistake box brings back missed questions after 1, 3 and 7 days, so the right pattern sticks.

Questions learners ask

Is "different than" wrong in American English?

No. Americans often say different than, especially before a clause: The base was different than I expected. But different from is correct in every kind of English, so it is the safest choice in writing and on a test. Different of and different with are always wrong.

Should I say "on the weekend" or "at the weekend"?

Americans say on the weekend, or on weekends for a habit: I work on weekends. At the weekend is British. The ECL and ALCPT use American English, so on is the choice to make. In the weekend is wrong in both kinds of English.

Do I need a preposition before "home"?

Not after verbs of movement: go home, come home, get home, arrive home. To say where someone is, Americans say at home or simply home: She's at home today, or She's home today. Never say go to home or arrive to home.

How can I learn so many fixed combinations?

Do not try to learn long lists at once. Pick five pairs a week, such as responsible for and depend on, and write one true sentence about your own life with each. Say them aloud, test yourself two days later, and add every pair you miss in practice to your list.
