Determiners practice: articles, quantifiers and uncountable nouns

Can you count the noun? That one question decides most determiners. Learn the patterns for articles and quantifiers, then try 15 new questions with full explanations.

Updated October 18, 2026

Determiners are the small words in front of a noun that tell you which one or how much: a truck, the truck, much fuel, a few drivers, each driver, neither truck. Most of them depend on one question: can you count the noun? Bags can be counted, but luggage cannot, so you say many bags but much luggage.

The ECL and the ALCPT are multiple-choice tests of listening and reading in American English, and determiner items are easy to miss because the choices look so similar: few and a few, little and a little, each and every. One small word can change the meaning. There's little coffee left is a warning; there's a little coffee left is good news. In the listening part, missing that difference can lead you to the wrong answer for a whole dialog.

This page gives you the rules in four short steps, the mistakes learners make most often, 15 new questions with explanations, and tips for test day. For more detail, read the lesson on nouns, articles and quantifiers, then keep practicing in the free trainer.

The rules in brief

Step 1: Can you count the noun?

Countable nouns have a plural and can take a or an: a backpack, three backpacks. Uncountable nouns have no plural and never take a or an. Learn the ones that look countable but are not: equipment, information, advice, luggage, baggage, furniture, news, homework, traffic, fuel. To count them, add a unit: a piece of advice, two pieces of luggage, a piece of equipment.

Some nouns can be both, with a change in meaning: She has a lot of experience (skill), but The trip was a great experience (one event).

Step 2: a, an, the or no article?

ChooseWhenExample
a / anone countable thing the listener does not know yet; a jobI need a charger. My aunt is an architect.
a or an?follow the first sound, not the letteran hour, an MRI, a uniform, a one-way ticket
thea specific thing both people know; the only one; with same, first, bestThe charger on my desk is broken. We sat in the same row.
no articleplural or uncountable nouns in general; meals, languages, sports; most cities and countriesTrucks need regular care. We had lunch early. She speaks Korean.

Step 3: How much or how many?

With plural countable nounsWith uncountable nouns
many: How many bags do you have?much: How much luggage do you have?
a few = some, a positive idea: I have a few questions.a little = some, a positive idea: Add a little sugar.
few = not many, a negative idea: Few people signed up.little = not much, a negative idea: We had little rain this summer.

These work with both kinds: some, any, enough, a lot of, plenty of, no, all, most. Use some in positive sentences and offers, and any in negatives and most questions: We have some water, but we don't have any ice. In positive sentences, Americans usually say a lot of, not much: We have a lot of work today. Plenty of means more than enough: Take one; we have plenty of pens. Put enough before a noun but after an adjective: enough chairs, big enough. For more, fewer and less, see comparisons.

Step 4: each, every, either, neither, both, all

  • each + singular noun, for two or more, one by one: Each driver signs the log. You can also say each of the drivers.
  • every + singular noun, for three or more, the whole group: Every room has a smoke alarm. Never every of; say every one of.
  • either / neither + singular noun, for two: You can sit at either table. Neither answer is right.
  • both + plural noun, for two: Both doors were locked. all, for three or more, or with uncountable nouns: All the equipment is new.

Common mistakes

  • Not: We need more equipments for the new gym. Say: We need more equipment for the new gym. Equipment is uncountable, so it never takes -s, a or an.
  • Not: My sergeant gave me a good advice. Say: My sergeant gave me some good advice. Advice is uncountable; for one idea, say a piece of advice.
  • Not: How much people work in your office? Say: How many people work in your office? People is a plural countable noun, so it takes many.
  • Not: I have little money, so I can lend you ten dollars. Say: I have a little money, so I can lend you ten dollars. Little means almost none, but a little means some, which matches the offer.
  • Not: Every of the rooms has a window. Say: Each of the rooms has a window. Every is never followed directly by of; use each of or every one of.
  • Not: He studies at an university in Texas. Say: He studies at a university in Texas. University starts with a "you" sound, and a or an follows the sound, not the letter.
  • Not: In my country, the people eat dinner late. Say: In my country, people eat dinner late. When you talk about people or things in general, use no article.

Practice questions

Choose an answer for each sentence, then open Show the answer to check it and read why the other choices are wrong.

  1. It was ______ honor to shake hands with the astronaut after her talk.

    • Aan
    • Ba
    • Cthe
    • D(no article)
    Show the answer

    A. an

    Choose a or an by the first sound, not the first letter. The h in honor is silent, so the word starts with a vowel sound and takes an, like an hour. A is tempting because h is a consonant letter, but we never pronounce it here.

  2. I'm surprised you brought so ______ luggage for a three-day trip.

    • Amany
    • Bfew
    • Cmuch
    • Da lot of
    Show the answer

    C. much

    Luggage is uncountable, like baggage and equipment, so it takes much: so much luggage, too much luggage. Many is the tempting choice, but it goes only with plural countable nouns, as in so many bags. A lot of cannot follow so, and few needs a plural noun.

  3. The new manager showed ______ interest in our ideas, so after a while we stopped sharing them.

    • Aa little
    • Blittle
    • Ca few
    • Dfew
    Show the answer

    B. little

    Little means not much, and it has a negative feeling: almost no interest, which explains why people stopped sharing. A little is the tempting choice, but it means some, a positive idea that does not match the second part. Few and a few need plural nouns.

  4. My wife and I went to ______ same high school, but we didn't meet until years later.

    • Aa
    • Ban
    • C(no article)
    • Dthe
    Show the answer

    D. the

    Same always takes the: the same school, the same day, the same mistake. A is tempting because the school is mentioned for the first time, but same points to one specific school that both people know, so the is the only choice.

  5. The nurse at the clinic gave me ______ useful information about healthy eating.

    • Asome
    • Ba
    • Cmany
    • Da few
    Show the answer

    A. some

    Information is uncountable in English: no plural, and no a or an. With uncountable nouns, use some, a lot of or a piece of. A is tempting because information can be counted in some other languages, but a useful information is wrong. Many and a few need plural nouns.

  6. My new boots were so stiff that I had a blister on ______ foot by the end of the day.

    • Aevery
    • Bboth
    • Ceach
    • Dall
    Show the answer

    C. each

    Each can refer to the members of a group of two, so each foot means both feet, one by one. Every needs a group of three or more, so every foot is wrong. Both and all need a plural noun: both feet.

  7. On weekdays the whole company has ______ lunch at 1130, so the line at the dining facility gets long fast.

    • Athe
    • B(no article)
    • Ca
    • Dan
    Show the answer

    B. (no article)

    Meals such as breakfast, lunch and dinner normally take no article: have lunch, after dinner. The is tempting, but it is only for one specific meal, as in The lunch at the wedding was great. A needs an adjective first: a quick lunch.

  8. ______ of my two brothers lives near me; one is in Alaska and the other is in Florida.

    • ANone
    • BEither
    • CBoth
    • DNeither
    Show the answer

    D. Neither

    For two people or things, the negative word is neither: not one and not the other. None is the tempting choice, but it is for groups of three or more. Either would mean that one of them lives near me, which the second part rules out.

  1. The new gym has ______ equipment, but most of it is still in boxes.

    • Amany
    • Ba few
    • Ca lot of
    • Dan
    Show the answer

    C. a lot of

    Equipment is uncountable, so in a positive sentence like this one, use a lot of or plenty of. Many is the tempting choice, but it needs a plural countable noun, and equipment has no plural. A few and an also need countable nouns.

  2. We don't have ______ milk left, so I'll stop at the store on the way home.

    • Aany
    • Bsome
    • Cno
    • Dmany
    Show the answer

    A. any

    In negative sentences, use any: we don't have any milk. Some is the tempting choice, but it is for positive sentences and offers, such as We have some milk. No is wrong because don't is already negative, and many does not go with an uncountable noun like milk.

  3. Very few of the new recruits knew how to swim. This tells you that:

    • Aa small but useful number could swim
    • Bnot many could swim, and that was a problem
    • Cmost of them could swim
    • Dnone of them could swim at all
    Show the answer

    B. not many could swim, and that was a problem

    Few, without a, means not many, and it sounds negative: the number was smaller than hoped. A small but useful number is the meaning of a few, with a. Few does not mean zero, so none of them could swim at all is also wrong.

  4. ______ of the two candidates have strong experience, so the board will have a hard time choosing.

    • AAll
    • BEach
    • CEither
    • DBoth
    Show the answer

    D. Both

    Both is for two people or things and takes a plural verb: both of the candidates have. All is the tempting choice, but it is for three or more. Each and either would need the singular verb has, so the verb have shows the answer.

  5. Let me give you ______ advice: always keep a copy of every form you sign.

    • Aa piece of
    • Ban
    • Cone
    • Da few
    Show the answer

    A. a piece of

    Advice is uncountable, so it never takes an, one or a plural word like a few. To talk about one idea, say a piece of advice, or simply some advice. An is the tempting choice because advice starts with a vowel, but an advice is always wrong.

  6. There aren't ______ chairs for everyone, so some people will have to stand in the back.

    • Amuch
    • Ba little
    • Cenough
    • Dplenty
    Show the answer

    C. enough

    Enough means as many as you need, and it works with plural and uncountable nouns: enough chairs, enough water. Much is the tempting choice, but chairs are countable. Plenty would need of, and it means more than enough, which does not fit a shortage.

  7. Relax, we have ______ time; the movie doesn't start until eight.

    • Amany
    • Bplenty of
    • Ca few
    • Dplenty
    Show the answer

    B. plenty of

    Plenty of means more than enough, and it works with both kinds of nouns: plenty of time, plenty of seats. Plenty without of is tempting, but before a noun it needs of. Many and a few do not fit an uncountable noun like time.

Tips for the test

  • Look at the noun first and ask two questions: can I count it, and is it singular or plural? The answers remove most wrong choices before you read them all.
  • Keep a short list of uncountable nouns that look countable: equipment, information, advice, luggage, furniture, news, homework. They take much, a little, some or a piece of, never a or -s.
  • Use the verb as a clue. Each, every, either and neither take a singular verb (each of them has); both takes a plural verb (both of them have), and so does all with a plural noun.
  • Read the rest of the sentence for the feeling. Few and little sound like a problem (so we canceled), while a few and a little sound like enough (so let's take a short break).
  • For a or an, say the word quietly to yourself: an hour, a uniform, an MRI. Your ear hears the sound that decides.

Questions learners ask

Is "less people" wrong?

In careful English, use fewer with plural countable nouns (fewer people, fewer mistakes) and less with uncountable nouns (less time, less traffic). Many Americans say less people in conversation, but in writing and on a test, choose fewer before a plural noun.

Can I use "much" in a positive sentence?

You can, but in conversation it often sounds formal or stiff. Americans usually say a lot of or plenty of in positive sentences: We have a lot of time. Keep much for questions and negatives (Do you have much time? We don't have much time) and for too much, so much and very much: We have too much work.

Is "news" singular or plural?

News is uncountable and takes a singular verb, even though it ends in -s: The news is good today. Do not say a news; say a piece of news or some news. A few school subjects work the same way, such as mathematics and physics: Physics is my favorite class.

Do country names take "the"?

Most do not: Canada, Japan, Mexico, Germany. Use the when the name is plural or includes a word like States, Kingdom or Republic: the United States, the Philippines, the United Kingdom, the Dominican Republic. Cities and states take no article either: Chicago, Texas.
