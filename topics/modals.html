Modal verbs practice: 15 questions on must, can and should

Fifteen new questions on obligation, ability, advice and how sure you are, now and in the past. Each answer explains why the right modal fits and why the tempting one does not.

Updated October 18, 2026

Modal verbs are small words with big meanings. You must go, you should go, you can go and you don't have to go look alike, but they send four different messages: an order, advice, permission and a free choice. Past forms such as must have, should have and needn't have add one more layer: they show how sure the speaker is about the past, or what they regret.

On the ECL and the ALCPT, modal questions often have four choices that are all real English, and only one fits the meaning of the sentence. The clue is usually in the other half: it's optional, his car is gone, she was in Ohio that week. In the listening part, you also need to catch small sounds, such as can versus can't, or should have said fast as "should've."

This page gives you the rules in short tables, common learner mistakes, 15 new questions with full explanations, and tips for the test. If you want the complete lesson first, read modal verbs, then come back and practice.

The rules in brief

A modal is followed by the base form of the verb: She can swim, not She can swims or She can to swim. Have to, need to and be able to work like normal verbs (he has to, did you have to?), so they fill the tenses that modals don't have.

Obligation, no obligation, prohibition

FormMeaningExample
muststrong obligation, often a written rule or the speaker's own viewAll visitors must sign in at the front gate.
have toobligation from outside: a rule, a boss, the situationI have to renew my license before May.
must not (mustn't)it is forbiddenYou must not park in front of a fire hydrant.
don't have to, don't need to, needn'tit is not necessary; you can chooseYou don't have to bring lunch; we're ordering pizza.
had to, will have topast and future obligation (must has no other forms)We had to wait two hours at the clinic.

Needn't is less common in American English, but you will meet it on tests. It means the same as don't have to.

Ability, permission, possibility

  • can / could / be able to for ability: My son can ride a bike now. I could read before I started school. For one success on one occasion in the past, use was able to or managed to: The firefighters were able to save the house. In the negative, couldn't is fine: I couldn't open the jar.
  • can / could / may for permission and requests, from informal to formal: Can I sit here? Could you hold this for a second? May I see your ID, please?
  • may / might / could for something possible: The meeting may run late. Take a coat; it might get cold tonight.

Advice

  • should / ought to: the right thing to do. You should drink more water in this heat. We ought to call Grandma this weekend.
  • had better ('d better) + base verb: strong advice with a warning. You'd better leave now, or you'll miss your flight.

How sure are you?

How sureAbout nowAbout the past
almost certain it is trueShe must be tired.She must have worked late.
possibleHe may / might / could be at lunch.He may / might / could have missed the bus.
almost certain it is not trueThat can't be Tom; he's in Ohio.He can't have / couldn't have seen us.

Past modals: modal + have + past participle

  • should have: a good idea that did not happen. I should have charged my phone.
  • could have: it was possible, but it didn't happen. You could have asked me for help.
  • needn't have: you did it, but it was not necessary. We needn't have cooked so much; half the guests didn't come. Didn't need to + verb only says it was not necessary; often you didn't do it: I didn't need to pay; my ticket was free.

Modals with have also build the third conditional (would have, could have), which you can practice on the conditionals practice page. In reported speech, must often becomes had to; see reported speech.

Common mistakes

  • Not: You must to show your ID at the gate. Say: You must show your ID at the gate. Modals such as must, can and should take the base form with no to.
  • Not: You mustn't wear a tie to the party; it's casual. Say: You don't have to wear a tie to the party; it's casual. Mustn't means it is forbidden, but here a tie is simply not necessary.
  • Not: Yesterday I must stay late at work. Say: Yesterday I had to stay late at work. Must has no past form, so use had to for a past obligation.
  • Not: After two hours, the mechanic could fix the engine. Say: After two hours, the mechanic was able to fix the engine. For one success on one occasion in the past, use was able to or managed to, not could.
  • Not: I will can help you tomorrow. Say: I will be able to help you tomorrow. Two modals cannot stand together, so after will use be able to.
  • Not: You had better to take a jacket. Say: You had better take a jacket. Had better looks past, but it gives advice about now or the future, and it takes the base form.
  • Not: She must have forget her keys. Say: She must have forgotten her keys. After must have, should have or could have, use the past participle.

Practice questions

Choose an answer for each sentence, then open Show the answer to check it and read why the other choices are wrong.

  1. You ______ bring a towel to the pool; the hotel gives you one at the door.

    • Amust
    • Bdon't have to
    • Cmustn't
    • Dcouldn't
    Show the answer

    B. don't have to

    The second half tells you a towel is not necessary, because the hotel gives you one. That is don't have to: no obligation, so you can choose. Mustn't is the tempting choice, but it means it is forbidden, and nobody is forbidding you to bring your own towel.

  2. Drivers ______ use a handheld phone in a school zone; it's against the law.

    • Amust not
    • Bdon't have to
    • Cneedn't
    • Dhave to
    Show the answer

    A. must not

    It's against the law shows a prohibition, so you need must not. Don't have to and needn't are tempting because they are negative too, but they only mean that something is not necessary, which is far too weak for a law.

  3. When my car broke down on the highway last week, I ______ call a tow truck.

    • Amust
    • Bhave to
    • Chad to
    • Dmust have
    Show the answer

    C. had to

    Last week shows a past obligation. Must has no past form, so English uses had to. Must is tempting because it expresses the same kind of obligation, but it only works for the present and the future. Must have would be a guess about the past, and it needs a past participle.

  4. ______ wear a uniform at your new job, or can you dress any way you like?

    • ADo you have to
    • BDo you must
    • CMust you to
    • DHave you to
    Show the answer

    A. Do you have to

    Have to works like a normal verb, so questions use do: Do you have to wear...? Do you must is wrong because must is a modal and never takes do. Must you to adds a to that must never takes, and Have you to is not used in American English.

  5. When I was a teenager, my parents let me borrow the car, but I ______ drive it after ten at night.

    • Amustn't
    • Bdidn't have to
    • Cneedn't
    • Dcouldn't
    Show the answer

    D. couldn't

    This is a past rule from the parents: driving after ten was not allowed. For a past prohibition, use couldn't or wasn't allowed to. Mustn't is tempting, but it has no past form. Didn't have to would mean that driving late was not necessary, not that it was forbidden.

  6. The surgery last night was difficult, but in the end the doctors ______ save his leg.

    • Acould
    • Bwere able to
    • Ccan
    • Dcould be able to
    Show the answer

    B. were able to

    This is one success on one occasion in the past, so use were able to (or managed to). Could is the tempting choice, but in a positive sentence it describes a general ability, not a single achievement. Can is present, and could be able to is not used here.

  7. I haven't ______ sleep well since we moved next to the highway.

    • Acould
    • Bcan
    • Cbeen able to
    • Dbe able to
    Show the answer

    C. been able to

    After haven't you need a past participle. Can has no participle, so English uses been able to. Could is tempting because it is the past of can, but haven't could is impossible. Be able to is the right verb in the wrong form.

  8. I'm not sure where Lisa is. She ______ be at the dentist; she said she had an appointment this week.

    • Amight
    • Bmust
    • Ccan't
    • Dhas to
    Show the answer

    A. might

    I'm not sure shows that the speaker is only guessing, so you need a modal of possibility: might (or may, could). Must is tempting because of the clue about the appointment, but must means you are almost certain, and the speaker says the opposite.

  1. You ______ ask your landlord before you paint the walls.

    • Aought
    • Bshould to
    • Cmust to
    • Dought to
    Show the answer

    D. ought to

    Ought to always keeps its to: You ought to ask. It means about the same as should, but should never takes to, so should to is wrong. The bare word ought is the tempting choice, but without to the sentence is incomplete. Must to has the same problem as should to.

  2. You ______ be late for your job interview; Mr. Lopez doesn't like to wait.

    • Ahad not better
    • Bhad better not
    • Chadn't better
    • Ddidn't have better
    Show the answer

    B. had better not

    Had better gives strong advice with a warning, and in the negative, not comes after better: had better not (often 'd better not). Hadn't better is the tempting choice because it looks like a normal negative, but it is not standard English. Didn't have better is never used.

  3. Nobody is answering the door, and the car isn't in the driveway. They must have gone out.

    • AThey were required to go out.
    • BMaybe they will go out later.
    • CI'm sure they went out.
    • DThey should go out.
    Show the answer

    C. I'm sure they went out.

    Here must have + past participle is a conclusion about the past: the evidence (no answer, no car) makes the speaker almost sure. It is not an obligation, so They were required to go out is the tempting but wrong reading. Must have looks back at something that very probably happened.

  4. Tom ______ my email yet; he has been in a meeting without his phone since nine.

    • Amust have read
    • Bshould have read
    • Cneedn't have read
    • Dcan't have read
    Show the answer

    D. can't have read

    The speaker is sure that something did not happen, so use can't have + past participle. Must have read is the tempting choice because must also shows certainty, but it means the opposite: I'm sure he read it. A meeting without a phone makes that impossible.

  5. I'm so sunburned. I ______ some sunscreen before we went to the beach.

    • Ashould have put on
    • Bmust have put on
    • Cshould put on
    • Dhad better put on
    Show the answer

    A. should have put on

    Should have + past participle shows a regret: putting on sunscreen was a good idea, but I didn't do it. Must have put on is tempting because it also looks back, but it is a conclusion that something happened, and the sunburn shows that it didn't.

  6. We ______ at a cheaper hotel, but we wanted to be close to the beach.

    • Amust have stayed
    • Bcan't have stayed
    • Ccould have stayed
    • Dshould stay
    Show the answer

    C. could have stayed

    Could have + past participle shows a past possibility that was not chosen: a cheaper hotel was an option, but we stayed near the beach. Must have stayed is tempting, but it is a guess about what probably happened, and the speaker knows exactly what happened.

  7. We needn't have bought so much food; only half of the guests came.

    • AWe weren't allowed to buy it.
    • BWe bought it, but it wasn't necessary.
    • CWe probably didn't buy it.
    • DWe didn't buy it because it wasn't necessary.
    Show the answer

    B. We bought it, but it wasn't necessary.

    Needn't have + past participle means you did something that turned out to be unnecessary: we bought the food, and then it was too much. The last choice is tempting, but it says the food was never bought, and needn't have always means the action happened.

Tips for the test

  • Find the clue in the other half. Words like it's optional, it's against the law, I'm not sure or his car is gone tell you the meaning you need: no obligation, prohibition, possibility or certainty. Choose the modal after you have found the clue.
  • Ask: a rule or a conclusion? Must can be an order (You must sign here) or a conclusion (You must be tired). A rule tells people what to do; a conclusion says what is probably true.
  • Check the time. The past usually needs a new form: had to for must, could or was able to for can, and modal + have + past participle for guesses and regrets.
  • Check the form. A modal takes the base verb, and modal + have takes the past participle, so choices such as must to go or should have went can be crossed out at once.
  • Listen for stress. In fast speech, should have sounds like "should've." To tell can from can't, listen to the stress: can is usually short and weak, while can't is stressed and longer.
  • Practice a little every day. The modals set in the free trainer explains each answer, and the mistake box brings back missed questions after 1, 3 and 7 days. For mixed grammar, try ECL grammar practice.

Questions learners ask

Is needn't used in American English?

Americans understand it, but in everyday speech they usually say don't have to or don't need to: You don't have to come. You will still see needn't and needn't have in formal writing and in test questions, so learn what they mean. When you speak or write, don't have to is the natural American choice.

Can must not mean "I'm sure it isn't true"?

Yes. In American English, must not is also used for a negative conclusion: The lights are off, so they must not be home. It means you are fairly sure because of the evidence. Can't is stronger and says something is impossible: That can't be Ana; she moved to Brazil last year. The context tells you whether must not is a rule or a conclusion.

What is the difference between must and have to?

Both express obligation. In everyday American speech, have to is the usual choice: I have to work Saturday. Must sounds formal or very strong, so you see it on signs, in rules and instructions, and when the speaker personally feels something is necessary: I must call my mother tonight. For the past and the future, use had to and will have to, because must has only one form.

Is should have the same as must have?

No. Should have + past participle is a criticism or regret: it was a good idea, but it didn't happen (You should have told me). Must have + past participle is a conclusion: I'm almost sure it happened (You must have told her; she already knows). If the sentence complains, think should have; if it explains evidence, think must have.
