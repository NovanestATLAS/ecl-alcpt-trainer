Military English vocabulary: 15 practice questions explained

Ranks and roles, daily life on base, military time read aloud, the phonetic alphabet and common abbreviations, each with a plain meaning and an example.

Updated October 18, 2026

If you train or work with Americans, you will hear words that a general English course rarely teaches: formation, chow, sick call, motor pool and times like "zero six hundred." Most of them name simple things. The trouble is that many look like ordinary words with a different meaning. On a base, a pass is not a mountain road, and your orders have nothing to do with a restaurant.

On a multiple-choice test, this costs points in two ways. In listening, you can understand every word of a short talk and still miss the answer because you did not catch that 1900 is 7 p.m. In reading, the tempting wrong choice is often a real word used in the wrong sense, like sick leave for sick call or carpool for motor pool.

This page gives you everyday service vocabulary in its American usage, grouped by theme, with a plain meaning and an example for each word. You will also find military time read aloud, the phonetic alphabet, common abbreviations, the mistakes learners often make and 15 practice questions with explanations. For other themes, such as travel, health and maps, see ECL vocabulary by theme.

Military English vocabulary by theme

Ranks and roles

WordMeaningExample
NCO (noncommissioned officer)a corporal or sergeant: an enlisted leaderAsk your NCO before you leave the building.
commanding officer (CO)the officer in command of a unitThe CO will speak at the graduation.
executive officer (XO)the second in command; takes over when the CO is awayThe XO is in charge today.
first sergeantthe senior NCO in a companyThe first sergeant inspects the rooms on Fridays.

Daily life on base

WordMeaningExample
formationsoldiers standing in lines to be counted or informedFormation is at 0545 outside the barracks.
duty rosterthe list of who has which duty, and whenCheck the duty roster for Sunday.
leavevacation days you earn, often used to travel homeShe took ten days of leave in July.
passshort time off, such as a night or a weekend; not counted as leaveHe has a weekend pass.
ordersan official document sending you to a new unit or baseMy orders came: I move in May.
briefinga short meeting that explains a task before it startsThe convoy briefing is at 0715.
chow(informal) food or a meal; the chow hall is the dining facilityWhat's for chow tonight?
barracksa building where soldiers live; always with -sOur barracks has a laundry room.
motor poolwhere a unit's vehicles are parked and repairedLeave the truck at the motor pool.
PX (Post Exchange)the store on an Army base (Air Force: the BX)The PX sells snacks, clothes and phones.
sick calltime to see a medic when you are sick or hurtGo to sick call if your ankle still hurts.

Military time, read aloud

Read the first two digits as the hour and the last two as the minutes. Say hundred only for 00 minutes. After noon, subtract 12.

WrittenSaidRegular time
0600zero six hundred6:00 a.m.
0815zero eight fifteen8:15 a.m.
1200twelve hundrednoon
1545fifteen forty-five3:45 p.m.
2000twenty hundred8:00 p.m.
2330twenty-three thirty11:30 p.m.

You will also hear oh for zero and hours at the end: "oh six hundred hours."

The phonetic alphabet

On the radio and the phone, letters are spelled with code words: Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliet, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar, Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray, Yankee, Zulu. Example: "My name is Park: Papa, Alpha, Romeo, Kilo."

Common abbreviations

AbbreviationMeaningExample
PTphysical trainingPT is at 0530 tomorrow.
HQheadquartersTake these forms to HQ.
ETAestimated time of arrivalOur ETA is 1615.
ASAPas soon as possibleThe XO wants the report ASAP.
AWOLabsent without leave: away without permissionHe was AWOL for two days.
MREmeal, ready-to-eat: a packaged field mealWe ate MREs in the field.

AWOL is said as one word ("AY-wall"); the others are usually said letter by letter.

Common mistakes

  • Not: The bus leaves at fourteen hundred thirty. Say: The bus leaves at fourteen thirty. Use hundred only when the minutes are 00, as in fourteen hundred.
  • Not: Nineteen hundred is 9 p.m. Say: Nineteen hundred is 7 p.m. Subtract 12 from any hour after noon: 19 minus 12 is 7.
  • Not: I have a pass to visit my parents for two weeks. Say: I have two weeks of leave to visit my parents. A pass is short, such as a night or a weekend; longer time off is leave.
  • Not: I went to sick leave this morning. Say: I went to sick call this morning. Sick call is the time to see a medic; sick leave is time off work, and you go on it, not to it.
  • Not: He lives in the new barrack. Say: He lives in the new barracks. The word always ends in -s, even when it means one building.
  • Not: I got my order to move to Alaska. Say: I got my orders to move to Alaska. When it means the document that sends you to a new place, the word is plural.
  • Not: The XO commands the unit, and the CO helps him. Say: The CO commands the unit, and the XO is second in command. CO means commanding officer; XO means executive officer, the number two.

Practice questions

Choose an answer for each sentence, then open Show the answer to check it and read why the other choices are wrong.

  1. Ortiz has been an NCO for six years, and the new soldiers in her squad often come to her for advice.

    • Aan officer who has just finished officer training
    • Ba civilian who works in the unit's office
    • Ca corporal or sergeant who leads enlisted soldiers
    • Da soldier who is still in basic training
    Show the answer

    C. a corporal or sergeant who leads enlisted soldiers

    NCO means noncommissioned officer: a corporal or a sergeant, an enlisted leader who trains and guides soldiers. The word "officer" makes the first choice tempting, but new officers are lieutenants. They hold a commission, so they are not NCOs.

  2. The commander is away at a conference this week, so the ______ is in charge of the battalion until she returns.

    • Acommanding officer
    • Bdrill sergeant
    • Cnew recruit
    • Dexecutive officer
    Show the answer

    D. executive officer

    The executive officer (XO) is the second in command and takes over when the commander is away. "Commanding officer" is the tempting choice, but the commanding officer is the commander, the person the sentence says is away. A drill sergeant trains recruits.

  3. At the end of formation, the first sergeant read the names of the soldiers on weekend duty.

    • Aa training course
    • Ba group of soldiers standing in ordered lines
    • Ca written report about the day
    • Da short break between classes
    Show the answer

    B. a group of soldiers standing in ordered lines

    A formation is a group of soldiers standing in ordered lines, where leaders count people and give out information, such as a list of names. In some languages a similar word means a training course, which makes the first choice tempting, but in English that is a course or training.

  4. Check the ______ before you make weekend plans. Your name might be on it for Saturday.

    • Aduty roster
    • Bchain of command
    • Csick call
    • Dmotor pool
    Show the answer

    A. duty roster

    A duty roster is the list that shows who has which duty and when, so you check it for your name. "Chain of command" is tempting because it is also about people, but it shows who gives orders to whom, not who works on Saturday.

  5. Private Hall wants to spend two weeks with his family in Ohio, so he has asked for ______.

    • Aa pass
    • Bleave
    • Csick call
    • Dhis orders
    Show the answer

    B. leave

    Leave is time off that counts as vacation days, so it is the right word for a two-week trip home. "A pass" is tempting, but a pass is short time off, such as a night or a weekend, and it would not cover two weeks.

  6. Sergeant Kim finally got his orders, and his family is moving to a base in Texas in June.

    • Apermission to take a long vacation
    • Ba promotion to a higher rank
    • Ca list of tasks for the next day
    • Dan official document that sends him to a new place or job
    Show the answer

    D. an official document that sends him to a new place or job

    A service member's orders are the official document that assigns him or her to a new unit, base or job, which explains the move to Texas. "Permission to take a long vacation" is tempting, but that is leave, and leave does not change where you serve.

  7. All drivers must attend a short safety ______ at 0700, before the convoy leaves the base.

    • Adebriefing
    • Bbriefcase
    • Cbriefing
    • Dbrochure
    Show the answer

    C. briefing

    A briefing is a short meeting that gives information or instructions before an activity, so it fits "before the convoy leaves." "Debriefing" is the tempting choice, but a debriefing comes after an activity, when people report what happened. A briefcase is a bag for papers.

  8. Go wash your hands, everybody. Chow is in ten minutes.

    • Aa meal
    • Ba short nap
    • Ca fitness test
    • Da room inspection
    Show the answer

    A. a meal

    Chow is informal American English for food or a meal, and the chow hall is where soldiers eat. The clue is "wash your hands," which you do before eating. A room inspection is tempting because it also needs preparation, but chow never means a check.

  1. If the truck makes that noise again, take it to the ______ and have a mechanic look at it.

    • Acarpool
    • Bmotor pool
    • Cmess hall
    • DPX
    Show the answer

    B. motor pool

    The motor pool is the area where a unit's vehicles are parked, checked and repaired, so mechanics work there. "Carpool" sounds close, but a carpool is a group of people who share rides to work. It is not a place where vehicles are fixed.

  2. I need a new phone charger, so I'll stop by the PX after work.

    • Athe post office on base
    • Bthe base clinic
    • Cthe store on an Army base
    • Dthe unit's main office
    Show the answer

    C. the store on an Army base

    PX stands for Post Exchange, the store on an Army base that sells everything from snacks to electronics. "Post office" is tempting because of the word post, but here post means the base itself, and you buy a charger in a store.

  3. My throat hurt so much this morning that I went to ______ instead of PT.

    • Asick leave
    • Broll call
    • Csick call
    • Da pass
    Show the answer

    C. sick call

    Sick call is the time when soldiers who are ill or hurt can see a medic or a doctor, so you go to sick call. "Sick leave" is the tempting choice, but it means days off work because of illness, and you go on sick leave, not to it.

  4. The bus to the airport leaves at 1430. In military time, you say that as ______.

    • Afourteen hundred thirty
    • Bfour thirty
    • Cone thousand four hundred thirty
    • Dfourteen thirty
    Show the answer

    D. fourteen thirty

    Read the first two digits as the hour and the last two as the minutes: fourteen thirty. Many learners add "hundred," but you say hundred only when the minutes are 00, as in fourteen hundred. "Four thirty" is wrong too, because 1430 is 2:30 p.m.

  5. The duty driver will pick you up at the main gate at nineteen hundred.

    • A7:00 p.m.
    • B9:00 p.m.
    • C9:00 a.m.
    • D7:00 a.m.
    Show the answer

    A. 7:00 p.m.

    Nineteen hundred is written 1900. After noon, subtract 12 from the hour: 19 minus 12 is 7, so it is 7:00 p.m. Many learners choose 9:00 p.m. because they hear "nine" in nineteen, but 9:00 p.m. is 2100, twenty-one hundred.

  6. On the radio, the sergeant spelled the name Ruiz: Romeo, Uniform, ______, Zulu.

    • AIndigo
    • BIndia
    • CItaly
    • DIgloo
    Show the answer

    B. India

    In the phonetic alphabet, I is India. The alphabet uses fixed code words so that letters stay clear on a bad radio line. Italy and Indigo are tempting because they begin with I, but they are not in the alphabet, so the listener could not be sure of the letter.

  7. Call me when you leave the gate and give me your ETA so I can meet you at the office.

    • Athe time you expect to arrive
    • Byour exact location
    • Cyour identification number
    • Dthe name of your driver
    Show the answer

    A. the time you expect to arrive

    ETA stands for estimated time of arrival: the time you think you will get somewhere. It is a time, not a place. "Your exact location" is tempting because the speaker wants to meet you, but to ask where you are, people say "Send me your location."

Tips for the test

  • Learn the look-alike pairs. Leave and pass, sick call and sick leave, briefing and debriefing, motor pool and carpool. In the questions on this page, the tempting wrong answer is often the other half of a pair like these.
  • Say times aloud. When you read 0715 on a schedule or on your phone, say it the military way: "zero seven fifteen." In listening, hundred tells you the minutes are 00.
  • Convert p.m. times fast. Subtract 12: 1600 is 4 p.m. and 2100 is 9 p.m. Practice until you do not need to count.
  • Spell with the phonetic alphabet. Spell your name, your email address and the license plates you see on the street. After a week, the 26 words come without thinking.
  • Listen for the clue. A hard word usually has a clue near it: a sore throat before sick call, hungry soldiers before chow. If you miss the word, the clue can still give you the answer. More ideas: how to practice listening.
  • Review what you miss. Use the flashcards on the vocabulary page, then try the ALCPT vocabulary test. In the free trainer, missed questions come back after 1, 3 and 7 days.

Questions learners ask

Is it "zero six hundred" or "oh six hundred"?

You will hear both. "Zero" is the careful, formal way. "Oh" is very common in everyday speech: "PT is at oh five thirty." Both mean the same time, so listen for the numbers, not the first word. Some speakers also add "hours" at the end, as in "thirteen hundred hours."

What is the difference between an officer and an NCO?

Officers, such as lieutenants, captains and majors, hold a commission and command units. NCOs, such as corporals and sergeants, are enlisted soldiers who were promoted to lead small teams and train soldiers. In the U.S. Army you salute officers but not NCOs, and you call a sergeant "Sergeant," not "sir" or "ma'am."

Why does the phonetic alphabet use words instead of letters?

Many letters sound alike, especially on a radio or a bad phone line: B, D, E, P and T, or M and N. A whole word such as Bravo or November is much harder to mishear. The same 26 words are used around the world by armed forces, pilots and air traffic controllers. Official lists spell two of them Alfa and Juliett.

Do I need these words if I am not in the military?

They help anyone who will study, train or work with Americans on a base, where you hear them every day. Some are everyday American English too: civilians also say ASAP, ETA and even chow. Knowing them also keeps you calm when a dialog takes place on a base, because you can focus on the question instead of a strange word.
