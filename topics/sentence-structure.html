Sentence structure practice: comparisons, word order and inversion

Faster or more fast? Where the office is or where is the office? Learn the rules behind these choices, see the mistakes learners really make, and test yourself on 15 new questions.

Updated October 18, 2026

Sentence structure is about two things: the right form of a word and the right order of the words. Many grammar items look simple. One word or a short group of words is missing, and all four choices seem possible. The difference is often a small detail, such as faster or more fast, where the office is or where is the office.

These patterns cost points because the wrong choices sound almost right. If you translate from your own language, you may pick an order that English does not allow. On a multiple-choice test, those "almost right" forms are often the ones placed next to the correct answer. The ECL and the ALCPT are multiple-choice tests of listening and reading in American English, so you need to spot the right form quickly, by eye and by ear.

This page covers comparatives and superlatives, as ... as, the more ... the better, word order in statements and embedded questions, the position of adverbs, inversion after negative words such as hardly and never before, and so or such ... that. You get short rule tables, real learner mistakes, 15 practice questions with explanations, and tips for the test. For the full lessons, read Adjectives, adverbs and comparisons and Questions, embedded questions and tag questions.

The rules in brief

Comparatives and superlatives

Use a comparative and than to compare two things. Use the and a superlative to compare one thing with a whole group.

Base formComparativeSuperlative
one syllable: fast, cheapfaster, cheaperthe fastest, the cheapest
one vowel + one consonant: big, hotbigger, hotterthe biggest, the hottest
ends in -y: busy, earlybusier, earlierthe busiest, the earliest
two or more syllables: reliablemore reliablethe most reliable
adverb in -ly: carefullymore carefullythe most carefully
irregular: good or well, bad, farbetter, worse, fartherthe best, the worst, the farthest

The new printer is more reliable than the old one. Friday is the busiest day at the PX. To make a comparative stronger, add much, far or a lot: much cheaper, never more cheaper.

Other comparison patterns

PatternExample
as + adjective + as (the same)At night the shuttle is as fast as a taxi.
not as + adjective + as (less)My new apartment isn't as big as the old one.
the + comparative, the + comparativeThe closer we got to the gate, the longer the line looked.
so + adjective or adverb + thatThe coffee was so hot that I couldn't drink it.
such (a/an) + adjective + noun + thatIt was such a long flight that we watched four movies.

Word order

  • Statements: subject + verb + object + place + time. We moved our office to the second floor last week.
  • Embedded questions: after Do you know, Can you tell me, I wonder or I'm not sure, use statement order and drop do, does and did. Where is the office? becomes Do you know where the office is? For a yes/no question, add if or whether: I'm not sure if the pool is open today.
  • Frequency adverbs (always, usually, often, never) go before the main verb, but after be and after the first helping verb: She always takes the stairs. He is never late.
  • Adverbs of manner go after the object, not between the verb and the object: He speaks Spanish very well.

Inversion after negative words

When a negative or limiting word starts the sentence, put the auxiliary (a helping verb such as have, had, can or will) before the subject, as in a question. If there is no auxiliary, add do, does or did. This style is formal.

StarterExample
Hardly / Scarcely ... whenHardly had we sat down when the fire alarm went off.
No sooner ... thanNo sooner had I bought the TV than it went on sale.
Never beforeNever before have I seen the gym so crowded.
Not only ... but alsoNot only did he fix the heater, but he also cleaned the vents.
Only then / Only afterThe nurse explained the risks. Only then did I sign the form.
Rarely / SeldomRarely does it snow here in April.

If the negative word is not at the start, keep normal order: We had hardly sat down when the alarm went off.

Common mistakes

  • Not: This road is more short than the highway. Say: This road is shorter than the highway. Short adjectives take -er, and more is only for longer adjectives like reliable.
  • Not: Her score was more better than mine. Say: Her score was much better than mine. Better is already a comparative, so you make it stronger with much, far or a lot.
  • Not: Do you know where is the dental clinic? Say: Do you know where the dental clinic is? In an embedded question, the subject comes before the verb, just as in a statement.
  • Not: I go always to the gym after work. Say: I always go to the gym after work. Frequency adverbs go before the main verb but after be, as in I am always tired on Mondays.
  • Not: Never I have seen such a long line at the post office. Say: Never have I seen such a long line at the post office. When never starts the sentence, the auxiliary must come before the subject.
  • Not: No sooner had we arrived when it started to rain. Say: No sooner had we arrived than it started to rain. No sooner goes with than, while hardly and scarcely go with when.
  • Not: It was so nice weather that we ate outside. Say: It was such nice weather that we ate outside. Use such before a noun and so before an adjective alone, as in The weather was so nice that we ate outside.

Practice questions

Choose an answer for each sentence, then open Show the answer to check it and read why the other choices are wrong.

  1. My cough is ______ today than it was yesterday, so I'm going to see the doctor.

    • Aworse
    • Bworst
    • Cmore bad
    • Dbadder
    Show the answer

    A. worse

    Bad is irregular: bad, worse, the worst. Than shows that you are comparing two days, so you need the comparative worse. Worst is the superlative, and it needs the, as in the worst day of the week. More bad and badder are not standard English.

  2. The new online booking system is ______ than the old phone line, so please use it for your appointments.

    • Amost reliable
    • Bmore reliable
    • Creliabler
    • Das reliable
    Show the answer

    B. more reliable

    Reliable is a long adjective, so its comparative is more reliable, followed by than. Most reliable is a superlative and needs the, as in the most reliable system on base. As reliable is wrong because as ... as must end with as, not than.

  3. That was ______ meal I've ever had at the dining facility.

    • Athe better
    • Bbest
    • Cthe best
    • Dthe most good
    Show the answer

    C. the best

    I've ever had compares this meal with every meal before it, so you need a superlative. Good is irregular (good, better, the best), and a superlative needs the. Best alone is missing the article, and the better is only for a choice between two things.

  4. The second week of the course wasn't ______ the first, so we had more free time in the evenings.

    • Aas busy than
    • Bbusier as
    • Cbusy as
    • Das busy as
    Show the answer

    D. as busy as

    Not as + adjective + as means less: the second week was less busy than the first. The two halves always match, as ... as. As busy than is the most tempting choice, but it mixes two patterns, because than belongs only after a comparative like busier.

  5. The earlier you get to the clinic, ______ you'll have to wait.

    • Aless
    • Bthe least
    • Cthe less
    • Dthe fewer
    Show the answer

    C. the less

    In the pattern the + comparative, the + comparative, both halves need the: the earlier you get there, the less you'll wait. Less without the breaks the pattern, and the least is a superlative. Fewer is for things you can count, like fewer people, not for an amount of waiting.

  6. Do you know ______ the shuttle to the airport leaves on Sundays?

    • Awhat time does
    • Bwhat time
    • Cwhat time is
    • Dat what time does
    Show the answer

    B. what time

    After Do you know, the question is embedded, so it takes statement word order and no do, does or did: what time the shuttle leaves. What time does is correct only in a direct question, such as What time does the shuttle leave on Sundays?

  7. Could you tell me how long ______? I need my car back by Friday.

    • Awill the repairs take
    • Bdo the repairs take
    • Ctake the repairs
    • Dthe repairs will take
    Show the answer

    D. the repairs will take

    Could you tell me introduces an embedded question, so the subject comes before the verb: the repairs will take. Will the repairs take is question order. It is right only in a direct question, such as How long will the repairs take?

  8. Our new instructor ______, so the lessons are easy to follow.

    • Aexplains the grammar very clearly
    • Bexplains very clearly the grammar
    • Cexplains the grammar very clear
    • Dvery clearly the grammar explains
    Show the answer

    A. explains the grammar very clearly

    An adverb of manner goes after the object, not between the verb and its object: explains the grammar very clearly. Explains very clearly the grammar follows the order of many other languages, but not English. Very clear is wrong because clear is an adjective and cannot describe a verb.

  1. Our neighbor ______ his music loud after ten at night; he's very polite.

    • Aplays never
    • Bnever play
    • Cnever plays
    • Ddoes never play
    Show the answer

    C. never plays

    Never is a frequency adverb, so it goes before the main verb: never plays. Plays never puts it after the verb, which is the most common mistake. Never play forgets the -s for he, and does never play adds a helping verb that the sentence does not need.

  2. Hardly ______ the car when my phone started ringing.

    • AI had parked
    • Bhad I parked
    • Cdid I park
    • Dhave I parked
    Show the answer

    B. had I parked

    Hardly at the start of a sentence needs inversion with the past perfect: had I parked, then when + past simple. I had parked is normal order, which is correct only if hardly moves to the middle: I had hardly parked the car when my phone started ringing.

  3. No sooner had the movie started ______ the power went out.

    • Awhen
    • Bthen
    • Cthat
    • Dthan
    Show the answer

    D. than

    No sooner contains the comparative sooner, so it pairs with than, just like earlier than or faster than. When is the most tempting choice, but when is the partner of hardly and scarcely, not of no sooner. Then and that cannot link the two parts.

  4. Never before ______ so many families at the base Fourth of July picnic.

    • Ahave we seen
    • Bwe have seen
    • Cwe saw
    • Dsaw we
    Show the answer

    A. have we seen

    When never before starts the sentence, the auxiliary comes before the subject: have we seen. We have seen is normal statement order. It works only if never moves after the auxiliary: We have never seen so many families at the picnic.

  5. Not only ______ the report late, but she also forgot to sign it.

    • Ashe submitted
    • Bshe did submit
    • Cdid she submit
    • Dsubmitted she
    Show the answer

    C. did she submit

    Not only at the start of a sentence needs inversion. The past simple has no auxiliary, so add did and use the base form: did she submit. She submitted is normal order, which is correct only when not only comes later: She not only submitted the report late but also forgot to sign it.

  6. Only when the rain stopped ______ start the barbecue.

    • Awe could
    • Bcould we
    • Cwe can
    • Dcan we
    Show the answer

    B. could we

    With only when, the inversion goes in the main clause, not in the when clause: Only when the rain stopped could we start. We could is normal order, which is right only if the sentence begins with the main clause: We could start the barbecue only when the rain stopped. Can does not match the past.

  7. It was ______ that we stayed on the beach until sunset.

    • Asuch a beautiful day
    • Bso beautiful day
    • Csuch beautiful day
    • Da so beautiful day
    Show the answer

    A. such a beautiful day

    Day is a singular countable noun, so the pattern is such a + adjective + noun: such a beautiful day. So beautiful day is the tempting choice, but so goes before an adjective alone: The day was so beautiful that we stayed. Such beautiful day is missing a.

Tips for the test

  • Find the signal word first. Than needs a comparative. A second as needs as before the adjective. Ever, of all or in the world usually point to a superlative.
  • Count the syllables. One syllable takes -er (cheaper), and three or more take more (more expensive). Never choose an option that uses both.
  • Check the first word of the sentence. If it is Hardly, No sooner, Never, Not only, Only then or Rarely, look for the auxiliary before the subject. If that word is in the middle, choose normal order.
  • A question inside a sentence sounds like a statement. After Do you know or Can you tell me, pick the choice with the subject before the verb and no do.
  • Say the pairs together: as ... as, so ... that, hardly ... when, no sooner ... than, not only ... but also. If you see one half, the other half tells you what goes in the blank.
  • Practice in short sets. Do 20 questions in the free trainer or on the ECL grammar practice page, and let the mistake box bring back the ones you missed. For more traps, read common grammar mistakes.

Questions learners ask

Should I say farther or further?

For physical distance, both are correct in American English, but farther is the more common choice: The pharmacy is farther than the PX. For the meaning more or extra, use further: further training, further questions. If you are not sure, use farther for miles and blocks, and further for ideas.

Do Americans really use inversion like "Hardly had I..." when they talk?

Not much in everyday conversation. People usually say "I had just sat down when the phone rang." Inversion is formal: you find it in news reports, speeches, official letters and some reading texts. You do not need to use it often, but you must recognize it, choose the right auxiliary, and match each starter with its partner word.

When does a comparative take the?

Usually it does not: This road is shorter. There are two exceptions. Use the with both comparatives in the double pattern: The sooner you book, the cheaper the ticket. Also use the when you choose between exactly two things: Of the two jackets, the black one is the warmer. With three or more, switch to the superlative.
