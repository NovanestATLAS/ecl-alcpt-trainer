Irregular verbs list: 112 verbs, easy patterns and 10 questions

Search the table for any verb, then learn the patterns behind the forms, from cut, cut, cut to grow, grew, grown, and test yourself with 10 questions.

Updated October 18, 2026

Every tense that looks back in time uses one of two verb forms: the simple past (V2) or the past participle (V3). For regular verbs, both end in -ed: I worked, I have worked. Irregular verbs have their own forms, and you simply have to know them: went and gone, wrote and written, brought and brought.

That is why tense questions depend on them. You can know that a sentence needs the present perfect and still lose the point by choosing has went instead of has gone. The past perfect (had begun), the passive (was stolen) and conditionals (if I had known) all use V3 too, so one weak verb can cost you points in several kinds of questions.

So how should you learn them? Not from top to bottom. Learn the verbs in groups that change the same way, say the three forms aloud as one unit ("sing, sang, sung") and use each verb in a short sentence about your own day. Ten verbs a day covers the whole table in under two weeks. This page gives you the table, the patterns, notes on American usage, common mistakes and 10 practice questions. For how the tenses work, see English verb tenses; for more past-tense practice, try simple past practice.

Patterns that make irregular verbs easier

The table looks long, but most verbs follow a few patterns. Learn each group together.

1. One form for all three: cut, cut, cut

Bet, cost, hit, hurt, let, put, quit, set, shut and spread never change. Most end in -t or -d. She hurt her knee yesterday, and it has hurt all week. Read is spelled the same, but its past and participle sound like "red."

2. Same past and participle: bring, brought, brought

This is the biggest group, so learn it in families:

  • -ought, -aught: bring, buy, fight, seek, think: brought, bought, fought, sought, thought. Catch, teach: caught, taught.
  • ee or ea becomes a short e: keep, sleep, sweep, feel, mean, deal, leave: kept, slept, swept, felt, meant, dealt, left. Meet, feed, bleed, lead, flee: met, fed, bled, led, fled.
  • -d becomes -t: bend, build, lend, send, spend: bent, built, lent, sent, spent.
  • a u sound: dig, hang, stick, sting, strike: dug, hung, stuck, stung, struck.
  • others: find/found, hold/held, sell/sold, tell/told, stand/stood, sit/sat, win/won, lose/lost, make/made, pay/paid, say/said, hear/heard.

I bought the tickets online and have already sent them to you.

3. i, a, u: sing, sang, sung

Begin, drink, ring, sink and swim work the same way: began/begun, drank/drunk, rang/rung, sank/sunk, swam/swum. The a form is the simple past; the u form comes after have, has, had or be. We swam in the lake on Sunday. I had never swum in water that cold.

4. -ew and -own: grow, grew, grown

Blow, know, throw and fly do the same: blew/blown, knew/known, threw/thrown, flew/flown. Draw and withdraw are close (drew/drawn, withdrew/withdrawn), and show mixes the systems: showed, shown. My son grew five centimeters this year; he has grown out of his shoes.

5. Past with o, participle with -en: speak, spoke, spoken

Also break, choose, freeze, steal, wake and forget (broke/broken, chose/chosen, froze/frozen, stole/stolen, woke/woken, forgot/forgotten), and write, drive, ride and rise (wrote/written, drove/driven, rode/ridden, rose/risen). Swear, tear and wear are close: swore/sworn, tore/torn, wore/worn. My bike was stolen on Friday.

In a few verbs the participle looks like the base form: come/came/come, become/became/become, run/ran/run. She has become a sergeant.

American English notes

  • gotten: the usual participle of get when it means receive, become or arrive: I've gotten three emails from the XO today. It has gotten cold. Have got stays for "have" and "must": I've got two kids. I've got to go.
  • dove: Americans often use dove as the past of dive: He dove into the pool. Dived is also correct and is the usual participle.
  • learned, burned: Americans prefer the regular forms: learned, burned, dreamed, spelled. The -t forms (learnt, burnt, dreamt) are mainly British, though burnt is common as an adjective: burnt toast. For burn, dream and learn, where the table shows two forms, choose the -ed form.

Common mistakes

  • Not: She has went to the clinic. Say: She has gone to the clinic. After have, has or had, use the participle (V3), not the simple past (V2).
  • Not: Did you saw the new schedule? Say: Did you see the new schedule? After did or didn't, the main verb goes back to the base form, because did already shows the past.
  • Not: He brang his own lunch. Say: He brought his own lunch. Bring belongs to the -ought family (bring, brought, brought), not to sing, sang, sung.
  • Not: Yesterday I drunk three cups of coffee. Say: Yesterday I drank three cups of coffee. In the i-a-u verbs, the a form is the simple past and the u form is the participle.
  • Not: I laid on the couch all afternoon. Say: I lay on the couch all afternoon. Lie (rest) goes lie, lay, lain; laid is the past of lay (put something down).
  • Not: Sergeant Brown teached us first aid. Say: Sergeant Brown taught us first aid. Teach and catch end in -aught and never take -ed.
  • Not: The price of gas has rised again. Say: The price of gas has risen again. Rise is irregular (rise, rose, risen), unlike raise, which is regular: raised.

Practice questions

Choose an answer for each sentence, then open Show the answer to check it and read why the other choices are wrong.

  1. Sergeant Lopez isn't in her office. She's ______ home for the day.

    • Awent
    • Bgone
    • Cgo
    • Dgoes
    Show the answer

    B. gone

    Here "she's" is short for "she has," so you need the past participle: she has gone. "Went" is tempting because the action is in the past, but went is the simple past, and the simple past never comes after have or has.

  2. Two hours before the march, Private Cole ______ a whole liter of water.

    • Adrank
    • Bdrunk
    • Cdrinked
    • Dhas drunk
    Show the answer

    A. drank

    This is a finished time in the past, so you need the simple past: drank. Drink follows the i-a-u pattern: drink, drank, drunk. "Drunk" is tempting, but it is the participle and needs have or had before it. "Has drunk" does not fit a finished past time.

  3. By the time we found the right building, the ceremony had already ______.

    • Abegan
    • Bbegin
    • Cbeginning
    • Dbegun
    Show the answer

    D. begun

    After had you need the past participle, and the participle of begin is begun: begin, began, begun. "Began" is the most tempting choice, but it is the simple past and cannot follow had. "Had already beginning" is not possible in English.

  4. I ______ my laptop to class yesterday, but I left the charger at home.

    • Abringed
    • Bbrang
    • Cbrought
    • Dbrung
    Show the answer

    C. brought

    Bring has the same form for the simple past and the participle: bring, brought, brought. "Brang" is tempting because it looks like sing, sang, but bring does not follow that pattern. "Bringed" and "brung" are not standard English.

  5. All the answers on this form must be ______ in black ink.

    • Awrote
    • Bwriting
    • Cwritten
    • Dwrited
    Show the answer

    C. written

    Must be + past participle makes a passive sentence, and the participle of write is written: write, wrote, written. "Wrote" is tempting, but it is the simple past and cannot follow be. Remember the double t in written.

  1. As soon as the instructor ______ the whistle, everyone stopped and turned around.

    • Ablowed
    • Bblew
    • Cblown
    • Dhas blown
    Show the answer

    B. blew

    This is a finished action in a past story, so you need the simple past: blew. Blow follows the -ew/-own pattern: blow, blew, blown. "Blown" is tempting, but it is the participle and needs have or be before it. "Blowed" is not a real form.

  2. Your pronunciation has ______ much better since you started practicing with the recordings.

    • Abecame
    • Bgetting
    • Cgets
    • Dgotten
    Show the answer

    D. gotten

    After has you need a past participle, and in American English the participle of get is gotten, especially when it means become: has gotten better. "Became" is tempting because the meaning is close, but it is the simple past. Its participle is become: has become better.

  3. After the long march, the soldiers ______ down on the grass and closed their eyes.

    • Alay
    • Blaid
    • Clied
    • Dlain
    Show the answer

    A. lay

    Lie, meaning rest your body flat, goes lie, lay, lain, so its simple past is lay. "Laid" is the most tempting choice, but it is the past of lay, to put something down, and it needs an object: they laid their bags down. "Lied" means said something untrue.

  4. Before last summer, my grandmother had never ______ on a plane.

    • Aflew
    • Bflyed
    • Cflown
    • Dfly
    Show the answer

    C. flown

    Had never + past participle is the past perfect, and the participle of fly is flown: fly, flew, flown, like grow, grew, grown. "Flew" is tempting, but it is the simple past and cannot follow had. "Flyed" is not a form of fly.

  5. The PX ______ the price of running shoes by twenty percent last week.

    • Ahas cut
    • Bcut
    • Ccuts
    • Dcutted
    Show the answer

    B. cut

    Cut has one form for all three: cut, cut, cut. With "last week" you need the simple past, which is simply cut. "Has cut" is tempting, but the present perfect does not go with a finished time like last week, and "cutted" does not exist.

Tips for the test

  • Look left of the blank first. Have, has, had or a form of be (is, was, been) means you need the participle. A finished time (yesterday, ago, last week) with no helper means the simple past. After did or didn't, use the base form.
  • Say the three forms as one word. "Go-went-gone" said aloud ten times stays longer than a list read once. Practice them in their pattern groups.
  • Be suspicious of strange -ed forms. Wrong choices often treat an irregular verb as regular: catched, bringed, teached. If an -ed form looks odd, check the table.
  • Choose the American form. In American English, expect gotten, learned and burned, and don't be surprised by dove.
  • Practice in context. Answer tense questions in the free trainer, where missed questions come back after 1, 3 and 7 days, then try tenses practice and ECL grammar practice.

Questions learners ask

How many irregular verbs do I need to know?

English has more irregular verbs than this table, but most of the others are rare or are built on shorter ones, like understand, overcome and withdraw. The 112 here are the ones you will meet most in everyday American English. Start with the most frequent: be, have, do, go, get, make, say, see, take, come, know, think, give, find and tell.

Is "have got" wrong in American English?

No. Americans say "I've got a car" (I have a car) and "I've got to leave" (I must leave). The difference is in the participle when get means receive, become or arrive: Americans say "I've gotten a letter" and "It's gotten late," where British speakers usually say got.

Why is "read" in the table if its forms do not change?

Because only the spelling stays the same. The base form sounds like "reed," but the past and the participle sound like "red": "I read the report yesterday." In listening, the sound tells you the tense, so say it aloud when you practice.
