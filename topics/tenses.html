Verb tenses practice: 15 questions on past, perfect and future

Most tense questions turn on one small clue: a time word like since or ago, a second action, or a word like when or by. Learn the clues, then test yourself on 15 new questions.

Updated October 18, 2026

A tense question gives you four forms of one verb, and usually only one of them fits. You don't have to guess or "feel" the answer. Almost every sentence contains a clue: a time word (since, ago, by Friday), a second action (when the phone rang), or a time clause (as soon as she arrives).

Tenses cost points on multiple-choice tests like the ECL and the ALCPT because the wrong choices are never random. They are the forms learners really mix up: I saw and I have seen, arrives and will arrive, did and was doing. In listening, the tense also tells you if something is finished, in progress or still a plan, and that difference often decides which answer is right.

This page gives you the rules on one screen, with a table of clues, the mistakes learners make most often, and 15 new questions. Each explanation tells you why the right form works and why the most tempting wrong one does not. For all twelve tenses in detail, read the verb tenses lesson.

The rules in brief

Find the clue first, then choose the tense. These are the clues you will meet most often.

Clue in the sentenceTenseExample
since, for (up to now), already, yet, ever, so far, this weekpresent perfect: have/has + past participleShe has lived on base since March.
yesterday, ago, last week, in 2019, questions with whensimple pastShe moved here two months ago.
when or while + an action in progresspast progressive: was/were + -ingI was jogging when it started to rain.
by the time, already (before another past action)past perfect: had + past participleThe bus had left by the time we got to the stop.
by + a future timefuture perfect: will have + past participleBy noon, we will have finished the inventory.
at 8 tonight, this time tomorrow, all day tomorrowfuture progressive: will be + -ingAt 8 tonight, I will be driving home.

Present perfect or simple past?

Use the present perfect when the time is not finished or not named: I have called the clinic twice today. Use the simple past as soon as the sentence names a finished time: I called the clinic twice yesterday. Since gives a starting point (since 2022, since I arrived); for gives a length of time (for three weeks). When the period is over, for takes the simple past: He worked in supply for six years (he doesn't work there now). The forms themselves are on the simple past practice page.

Two actions in the past

A longer action in progress takes the past progressive; the short action that interrupts it takes the simple past: We were eating lunch when the fire alarm went off. When one action was finished before another, use the past perfect for the earlier one: When I got to the office, my boss had already gone home. Past participles like gone, drunk and flown are in the irregular verbs table.

Talking about the future

  • will: a decision you make while speaking, a promise or an offer: I'll carry that box for you.
  • be going to: a plan made earlier, or a prediction from what you can see: We're going to repaint the kitchen in May. Look at that line. We're going to be late.
  • present progressive: an arrangement with a time or place: I'm seeing the dentist on Thursday.
  • present simple: timetables and schedules: The shuttle leaves at 7:15.
  • will be + -ing: in progress at a future moment: Don't call at nine. I'll be teaching a class.
  • will have + past participle: finished before a future moment: By June, she will have saved enough for a car.

Time clauses: no will after when

After when, after, before, as soon as, until and once, use a present tense for the future, and keep will in the main clause: As soon as the results come in, I will email you. Use the present perfect when the first action must be complete: You can go home when you have signed the form. If works the same way, as the conditionals lesson shows.

Common mistakes

  • Not: I have seen your brother at the gym yesterday. Say: I saw your brother at the gym yesterday. A finished time like yesterday never goes with the present perfect.
  • Not: I work in this office since 2021. Say: I have worked in this office since 2021. Since connects a past start to now, and only the present perfect can do that.
  • Not: I know my roommate for five years. Say: I have known my roommate for five years. A period up to now needs the present perfect, and a state verb like know does not take -ing.
  • Not: When my phone rang, I took a shower. Say: When my phone rang, I was taking a shower. The shower was already in progress, while I took a shower means you showered after the call.
  • Not: When we got to the theater, the movie already started. Say: When we got to the theater, the movie had already started. The movie started first, so the earlier action takes the past perfect.
  • Not: I'll call you when I will land in Dallas. Say: I'll call you when I land in Dallas. A time clause after when, as soon as or until uses the present simple for the future.
  • Not: By the time you get home, I will cook dinner. Say: By the time you get home, I will have cooked dinner. By the time + a future moment means finished before it, so use the future perfect.

Practice questions

Choose an answer for each sentence, then open Show the answer to check it and read why the other choices are wrong.

  1. Sergeant Ruiz ______ at the base clinic since she came back from Italy.

    • Aworks
    • Bhas worked
    • Cworked
    • Dis working
    Show the answer

    B. has worked

    Since gives the starting point of a situation that is still true, so the main verb takes the present perfect: has worked. Works is the tempting choice, but the present simple cannot link a past start to now. Notice that the since clause itself stays in the simple past: since she came back.

  2. We ______ into this apartment three weeks ago, and we still haven't unpacked all the boxes.

    • Ahave moved
    • Bhad moved
    • Cmoved
    • Dwere moving
    Show the answer

    C. moved

    Ago always marks a finished time, so the simple past is correct: moved. Have moved is tempting because the second half of the sentence uses the present perfect, but the present perfect never goes with ago. Each verb follows its own time word.

  3. We ______ dinner when the power went out, so we finished eating by candlelight.

    • Awere having
    • Bhad
    • Chave had
    • Dhad had
    Show the answer

    A. were having

    Dinner was already in progress when the short event happened, so it takes the past progressive: were having. Had is tempting, but the simple past would mean dinner started when the power went out, and had had would mean dinner was already over.

  4. A: There's no milk left in the fridge. B: Oh, I didn't know that. I ______ some on my way home.

    • Awill get
    • Bam getting
    • Cget
    • Dhave got
    Show the answer

    A. will get

    B decides at the moment of speaking, so will is the right form: I will get some. Am getting is tempting because it can talk about the future, but the present progressive is for an arrangement made earlier, and B has only just heard about the milk.

  5. The forms are due on Friday, but don't worry. By Thursday afternoon, I ______ all of them.

    • Acomplete
    • Bhad completed
    • Chave completed
    • Dwill have completed
    Show the answer

    D. will have completed

    By + a future time means the action will be finished before that moment, so we need the future perfect: will have completed. Had completed is the trap. The past perfect is for an action before a past moment, and Thursday afternoon is still in the future.

  6. I ______ my new ID card yet, so I still have to sign in at the front gate every morning.

    • Ahaven't received
    • Bdon't receive
    • Chadn't received
    • Dam not receiving
    Show the answer

    A. haven't received

    Yet in a negative sentence about the situation now takes the present perfect: haven't received. Hadn't received looks similar, but the past perfect needs an earlier moment in the past, and this sentence is about now (I still have to sign in).

  7. By the time we got to the store, it ______ out of bottled water because of the storm warning.

    • Aran
    • Bhas run
    • Chad run
    • Druns
    Show the answer

    C. had run

    By the time + a past event means the other action was complete before that moment, so we need the past perfect: had run out. Ran is the tempting choice, but the simple past does not show that the water was gone before you arrived.

  8. ______ anything this Saturday? A few of us are driving up to the lake.

    • ADo you do
    • BAre you doing
    • CDid you do
    • DHave you done
    Show the answer

    B. Are you doing

    Questions about plans and arrangements use the present progressive: Are you doing anything this Saturday? Do you do is a common mistake. The present simple asks about habits (What do you do on weekends?), not about one particular day in the future.

  1. My grandfather ______ for the railroad for thirty years before he retired in 2005.

    • Ahas worked
    • Bworked
    • Cworks
    • Dwas working
    Show the answer

    B. worked

    He retired in 2005, so the thirty years are over: use the simple past, worked. Has worked is the trap. For + a period of time takes the present perfect only when the period continues up to now, and his job ended long ago.

  2. I'll text you as soon as the doctor ______ me the results of the blood test.

    • Awill give
    • Bwould give
    • Cgave
    • Dgives
    Show the answer

    D. gives

    After as soon as, when, before and until, English uses a present tense for a future action, even when the main clause has will: as soon as the doctor gives. Will give is the classic mistake. Keep will in the main clause only.

  3. While Dana was running around the track, she ______ her ankle on a loose stone.

    • Awas twisting
    • Bhas twisted
    • Ctwists
    • Dtwisted
    Show the answer

    D. twisted

    The running is the longer background action (was running). The sudden event that interrupts it takes the simple past: twisted. Was twisting is tempting because of while, but you twist an ankle in a second, not over a period of time.

  4. Our flight ______ at 6:40 tomorrow morning, so we need to leave the house by 4:30.

    • Adeparts
    • Bhas departed
    • Cwill have departed
    • Ddeparted
    Show the answer

    A. departs

    Flights, trains and classes run on a timetable, so the present simple can describe a future time: departs at 6:40. Will have departed is tempting because it looks like a future form, but the future perfect needs by and means finished before then.

  5. You don't need to remind Kim about the briefing. I ______ her about it.

    • Ahad already told
    • Balready tell
    • Cam already telling
    • Dhave already told
    Show the answer

    D. have already told

    The action is finished and its result is true now (Kim knows), so we use the present perfect: have already told. Had already told is tempting because of already, but the past perfect needs a later past action to compare with, and there is none here.

  6. Watch out! That ladder is leaning too far to the left. It ______.

    • Afalls
    • Bhas fallen
    • Cis going to fall
    • Dfell
    Show the answer

    C. is going to fall

    You can see the danger right now, so the prediction takes be going to: it is going to fall. Falls is wrong because the present simple is for facts and habits, and has fallen would mean the ladder is already on the ground.

  7. Please don't call me between two and four tomorrow. I ______ my driving test then.

    • Awill have taken
    • Bwas taking
    • Cwill be taking
    • Dhave taken
    Show the answer

    C. will be taking

    Between two and four is a period in the future, and the test will be in progress during that time, so use the future progressive: will be taking. Will have taken is tempting, but it means the test is already over, and then a call would be no problem.

Tips for the test

  • Find the clue before you read the choices. Look for since, for, ago, yesterday, by, while, when and as soon as. The clue often removes two choices at once.
  • Ask if the time is finished. At 10 a.m. you can say I have had two cups of coffee this morning. At 3 p.m. the morning is over, so say I had two cups of coffee this morning.
  • With two past actions, ask which came first or which was in progress. The earlier one may need had + past participle; the one in progress needs was/were + -ing.
  • Cross out will after when, until and as soon as. One exception: I don't know when the bus will arrive is correct, because it means "I don't know the answer to the question When will the bus arrive?"
  • In listening, let the tense tell you the status. I've already sent it means it is done; I'm going to send it means it is not. A choice that says the report is finished is wrong in the second case.
  • Practice with feedback. The free trainer explains every answer, and its mistake box brings back missed questions after 1, 3 and 7 days.

Questions learners ask

Can I say "Did you eat yet?" in American English?

Yes. In everyday American English, people often use the simple past with already, yet and just: Did you eat yet? I already did it. The present perfect is also correct, and it is the safer choice on a test: Have you eaten yet? What is wrong in every kind of English is the present perfect with a finished time: not I have eaten an hour ago, but I ate an hour ago.

Do I always need the past perfect for the earlier of two past actions?

No. When before or after already shows the order, the simple past is fine too: After she finished her shift, she drove home. You need the past perfect when the order would not be clear without it, and it is the normal choice after by the time: By the time we arrived, the bus had left. In a test question, by the time + a past event is a strong signal for had + past participle.

What is the difference between will and be going to?

Use will for a decision you make while you speak (I'll take the stairs), for promises and offers, and for many predictions. Use be going to for a plan you made earlier (I'm going to apply for the course in May) and for a prediction based on something you can see now (Look at the ice. Someone is going to slip). For many predictions both are fine, so look for a clue: a sudden decision points to will, visible evidence or an earlier plan to going to.
