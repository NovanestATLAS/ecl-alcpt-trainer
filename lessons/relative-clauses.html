Relative clauses: who, which, that, whose, where

A relative clause (also called an adjective clause) gives more information about a noun. It starts with a relative pronoun and comes right after the noun it describes.

Relative pronouns

PronounRefers toExample
whopeople (subject or object)The soldier who won the prize is from Ohio.
whompeople (object, formal)The officer whom you met is my uncle.
whichthings and animalsThe truck which broke down is ours.
thatpeople or things (defining clauses only)The book that you borrowed from the library is overdue.
whosethe owner (people or things)The man whose car was stolen called the police.
whereplacesThat's the base where I trained.
whentimesI remember the day when we met.
whyreasons (after the reason)That's the reason why he left.

Common mistakes

  • ✗Wrong: The man which lives next door is a pilot.✓Right: The man who lives next door is a pilot.
  • ✗Wrong: The girl who her father is a pilot is in my class.✓Right: The girl whose father is a pilot is in my class.

Leaving out the pronoun

When the relative pronoun is the object of the clause (another subject follows it), you can leave it out of a defining clause.

  • The man (who) I saw was tall. → The man I saw was tall.
  • The map (that) you gave me is wrong. → The map you gave me is wrong.

You cannot leave it out when it is the subject: "The man who called you is here" (not The man called you is here).

Common mistakes

  • ✗Wrong: The man who I saw him was tall.✓Right: The man who I saw was tall.

Defining and non-defining clauses

DefiningNon-defining
identifies which person or thing: needed for the meaningadds extra information: the sentence makes sense without it
no commascommas around the clause
that is possiblethat is not possible: use who or which
The soldiers who passed the test went home early. (only those soldiers)My brother, who lives in Chicago, is a pilot. (I have one brother.)

Which can also refer to a whole idea: "He scored 90 on the ECL, which surprised everyone."

Prepositions in relative clauses

InformalFormal
the man (who) I spoke tothe man to whom I spoke
the house (which) we lived inthe house in which we lived (= where we lived)

After a preposition, use whom or which, never who or that: "the officer to whom I reported".

Shortening (reducing) relative clauses

You can shorten a relative clause by removing the pronoun and be, or by using an -ing or past participle form.

Full clauseReduced
The man who is standing by the door is my boss.The man standing by the door is my boss.
Soldiers who want to apply should see the clerk.Soldiers wanting to apply should see the clerk.
The car which was parked outside was towed.The car parked outside was towed.
The report that was written by the major is excellent.The report written by the major is excellent.
Paris, which is the capital of France, is beautiful.Paris, the capital of France, is beautiful.

ECL tipAn -ing form has an active meaning (the man stands). A past participle has a passive meaning (the car was parked).

How the ECL and ALCPT test relative clauses

Relative clause items ask you to join a noun to the information after it. Look at the noun just before the blank: a person takes who (or that), a thing takes which (or that), possession takes whose, a place takes where, and a time takes when. Then check the comma: after a comma, that is never possible.

Practice itemAnswerWhy
The officer ___ car was stolen called the police.whoseThe car belongs to the officer: possession is whose + noun, for people and for things.
This is the hangar ___ the helicopters are repaired.whereA place, and the clause already has its subject and verb. Which would need a preposition: in which the helicopters are repaired.
My sister, ___ lives in Texas, is a nurse.whoA comma means extra information about a person: who. That cannot follow a comma.
I will never forget the day ___ we arrived at the base.whenA time noun (day, year, moment) takes when.

If the blank is followed directly by a verb (who helped me), the pronoun is the subject and cannot be left out. If it is followed by a new subject (the book I bought), you may leave it out.

Tricky cases

Learners often mix these up; the test likes them too.

  • What means the thing that and has no noun before it: Tell me what you need. Compare: the things that you need.
  • Which after a comma can refer to a whole idea: He passed the test on the first try, which surprised everyone.
  • Formal English puts the preposition before whom or which: the officer to whom I spoke. Everyday English moves it to the end: the officer I spoke to.
  • The relative pronoun replaces the object, so do not repeat it: the book that I bought, not the book that I bought it.

Common mistakes

  • ✗Wrong: The man which helped me was a medic.✓Right: The man who helped me was a medic.
  • ✗Wrong: This is the book what I read.✓Right: This is the book that I read.
  • ✗Wrong: This is the city where I was born in.✓Right: This is the city where I was born.
